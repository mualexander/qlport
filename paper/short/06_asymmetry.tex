\section{The Asymmetry}
\label{s:sec:asymmetry}

Put the two directions beside each other. Forward, $96.6\%$ of occurrences translate, the
shortfall is five named gap codes, and every candidate for inexpressibility was removed by
writing the code or by exhibiting the expression --- bar $14$ occurrences left undecided.
A team that wants to leave is looking at work: unglamorous, enumerable, and finite.
Backward, a tag filter must become a glob; $91.9\%$ of concrete matchers have one, two
thirds of those only as a union --- $41$ branches for the commonest case and $206$ at the
widest this corpus needs --- and the residue divides into refusals that rest on a bound we
chose and refusals that rest on a theorem. A team that
wants to come back is looking at expressiveness it cannot buy.

That is the asymmetry, and the two halves are not symmetric in kind. ``Work remains''
is a statement about this translator, and Remark~\ref{s:rem:refusal} says such a statement
is weak evidence about a language. ``No glob denotes this language'' is a statement about
globs, and no amount of engineering changes it. The reverse direction is the only part of
this work where the refusals have that stronger warrant, which is why it is the part this
version keeps.

\paragraph{What would have to be true for this to be wrong.}
Three things, and each is worth stating because each is reachable. First, the forward
figure is a resubstitution estimate on a corpus the grammar was fitted to; the held-out
run returns $96.7\%$, but on an effective two dashboards' worth of independent variation.
A forward corpus with genuinely different conventions could lower it, though to overturn
the asymmetry it would have to lower it to where the shortfall contained a demonstrated
inexpressibility, which no evidence here suggests. Second, the reverse direction has no
translator behind it. Its numbers come from deciding the PromQL half of the corpus against
the glob target, not from running a converter anybody can use, so they describe what a
converter could achieve rather than what one does. Third, the ten unconditional refusals
are ten; a different corpus could contain none, and the theorems would still hold while the
asymmetry they support would be a smaller empirical fact.

\paragraph{What this version leaves out.}
One finding of the complete paper is deliberately absent here, and it is the largest one,
so this section names it rather than letting its absence pass for its non-existence. A
dashboard query does not determine its own meaning. $97.0\%$ of our translations depend on
a rollup interval the source query leaves to the dashboard's time range, and for the
$6.0\%$ of those where a widget pins that range itself, the interval a translator would
obviously supply is wrong two times in three. The metric's type is a second thing no query
states, and the one that can be checked against the vendor's own declarations: there our
inference is wrong for $6.5\%$ of the cases it can settle, $491$ occurrences. Nor is this
one vendor's doing --- $88.6\%$ of Datadog and $82.5\%$ of PromQL query occurrences carry a
template variable, a share that falls to $77.0\%$ on dashboards by other authors, so read
it as a property of this corpus rather than of the tools. What a migration ports is a query
schema plus a resolution layer belonging to the dashboard tool.

That finding is invisible to everything this version reports. A query that does not fix its
own meaning parses, translates, and is counted as covered, so neither $\kappa$ nor the gap
catalogue nor the grammars can see it; coverage and correctness differ by a measured amount
that no coverage number can detect. It does not contradict the asymmetry --- it sits beside
it, and it is a cost on the forward direction that the forward direction's own instruments
report as success. A reader who takes $96.6\%$ as the practical difficulty of leaving will
have taken the wrong number, and the complete paper is where that case is made.

\paragraph{What is measured, and what is not.}
We measure the query layer only. The corpus is queries extracted from published dashboard
and alerting documents, which is a sample of what people are willing to publish and not of
what a private estate contains. The reverse direction is evidence about tag filtering drawn
from the other half of that corpus. Stored history, alert evaluation semantics, dashboard
rendering and the cost of retraining the people who read the dashboards are all outside
it, and the last of those may well dominate the switching cost in practice.
