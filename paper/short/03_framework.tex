\section{Formal Framework}
\label{s:sec:framework}

``Are these languages inter-translatable'' is not yet a question. It becomes one once
four things are fixed: what the systems store, what a query denotes on what they store,
when two denotations count as the same, and what a translator is permitted to do when it
cannot proceed.

\begin{problem}
Let $L_s$ and $L_t$ be query languages over time-series databases related by an
ingestion mapping rather than shared. Determine the largest fragment of $L_s$ that admits
a meaning-preserving translation into $L_t$, exhibit the obstructions outside it, and
measure what share of queries written in practice the fragment contains.
\end{problem}

\noindent
Three features of that statement shape everything after it. It asks for a
\emph{fragment}, so the answer is a set of queries and not a yes or no. It asks for the
obstructions, so a translator that fails must say why in a form that can be counted. And
it asks what share of \emph{practice} the fragment covers, which makes the corpus part of
the result rather than an illustration of it.

\subsection{What the systems store}

\begin{definition}[Series, database, result]\label{s:def:model}
A \emph{series} is a pair $s=(\lambda,\langle (t_1,v_1),\dots,(t_n,v_n)\rangle)$ of a
finite label set $\lambda$ --- a partial map from label names to strings --- and samples
at strictly increasing times $t_i\in\mathbb{T}$ with values $v_i\in\mathbb{R}$. A series
carries a \emph{kind}: $\mathsf{counter}$ for a cumulative total, $\mathsf{delta}$ for a
per-interval increment, $\mathsf{gauge}$ otherwise. A \emph{database} $D$ is a finite set
of series with pairwise distinct label sets, and $\mathcal{D}$ is the class of all
databases. A \emph{result} is a finite partial map from label sets to $\mathbb{R}$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Two choices here are not neutral. Requiring distinct label sets makes a label set the
identity of a series, which is what both systems assume and what lets a result be keyed
by one. And the kind is part of the series, not of the query: a Datadog query does not
record it, and the translation depends on it.

\subsection{Queries, denotations, translators}

A query language is a set of strings with a meaning. We take the set to be generated by a
grammar, which is what makes the grammar question one about formal languages rather than
about parsers, and the meaning to be a function of a database and an instant.

\begin{definition}[Query language]\label{s:def:lang}
A \emph{query language} is a pair $(G,\sem{\cdot})$ of a context-free grammar $G$ and a
denotation $\sem{\cdot}:\Lang(G)\times\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{T}\to\mathit{Result}$. We
write $\Lang(G_{\mathrm{DD}})$ and $\Lang(G_{\mathrm{PQ}})$ for the two languages and
$\sem{\cdot}_{\mathrm{DD}}$, $\sem{\cdot}_{\mathrm{PQ}}$ for their denotations.
\end{definition}

\noindent
One thing this framework declares and does not define, and the paper is better for saying
so here than for leaving a reader to notice. The two denotations are \emph{declared}: no
definition of $\sem{\cdot}_{\mathrm{DD}}$ or $\sem{\cdot}_{\mathrm{PQ}}$ appears
anywhere, and the only denotation written out is the intermediate representation's. So
every soundness statement in this paper is an equation between two functions pinned down
by measurement rather than by definition --- the complete paper puts Datadog's bucket
boundaries, its reset handling and the way it pairs operands to a live account, and takes
PromQL's side from the published specification. Vendor semantics have to come from
somewhere and a measured semantics is the honest source, but the theorems are conditional
on those measurements.

\begin{definition}[Translator]\label{s:def:translator}
A \emph{translator} is a total function $T:\Lang(G_s)\to\Lang(G_t)\cup\{\bot\}$, where
$\bot$ carries a \emph{gap code} naming the construct that stopped it. Its \emph{domain}
is $\mathrm{dom}(T)=\{q : T(q)\neq\bot\}$, the fragment of the Problem Statement. The
translator this paper builds factors through an intermediate representation, so it is
useful to name the halves: a \emph{lowering} $L:\Lang(G_s)\to\mathit{IR}\cup\{\bot\}$ and
an \emph{emission} $E:\mathit{IR}\to(\Lang(G_t)\times\mathcal{P}(\text{codes}))
\cup\{\bot\}$, with $T=\pi_1\circ E\circ L$ and $\bot$ absorbing.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Totality is the substantive requirement. A translator that may crash, or silently emit
something plausible, cannot be measured: the fragment it covers would be whatever it
happened to survive. Requiring every failure to be a $\bot$ with a code makes
$\mathrm{dom}(T)$ observable and makes the complement a \emph{catalogue} rather than a
list of bug reports. A query is abandoned at its first obstruction, so the codes count
first refusals.

\begin{remark}[A refusal is not evidence by itself]\label{s:rem:refusal}
The discipline has a failure mode this work walked into and now reports. A gap code looks
like a statement about the languages, and it is in the first instance a statement about
$T$: the construct stopped \emph{this} translator. The two coincide only when something
independent says so --- a proof that no target expression exists, or a decision procedure
that agrees, or an implementation that removes the refusal and so shows it was never a
gap. This is the single most load-bearing piece of method here, and it is why
\S\ref{s:sec:regular} is the section this version keeps: it is the one place in the work
where the refusals have the first kind of warrant.
\end{remark}

\subsection{When two results agree, and what soundness means}

Comparing results needs a distance, not an equality test: the two systems compute in
floating point over different code paths, so bit equality would report disagreement where
none is meant. Relative error is the right scale, because the quantities span many orders
of magnitude and an absolute tolerance would be generous for rates and absurd for counts.

\begin{definition}[Result distance]\label{s:def:d}
For $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$ put $\delta(a,b)=|a-b|/\max(|a|,|b|)$, with $\delta(0,0)=0$. For
results $r_1,r_2$,
\[
d(r_1,r_2)=\begin{cases}
0 & \mathrm{dom}(r_1)=\mathrm{dom}(r_2)=\emptyset\\
\max_{\lambda\in\mathrm{dom}(r_1)}\delta\big(r_1(\lambda),r_2(\lambda)\big)
  & \mathrm{dom}(r_1)=\mathrm{dom}(r_2)\neq\emptyset\\
\infty & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
\end{definition}

\noindent
The first case is not a convention chosen for tidiness: two results that both select
nothing agree, and a maximum over the empty set would otherwise leave $d$ undefined on
exactly the pairs the failure modes of ingestion produce --- an empty window, a bucket
with no samples, a series that has not started. Differing label sets give $\infty$ rather
than a large number because they are a different kind of error: the translation returned
the wrong \emph{series}, and no tolerance should absorb that. The complete paper shows
$\delta$ is a metric on $\mathbb{R}$ and $d$ an extended metric on results, which it
needs because its admissibility argument is a triangle-inequality argument.

\begin{definition}[Exact and $\varepsilon$-sound]\label{s:def:sound}
Let $\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{D}$ and let $u:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}$ be an
\emph{evaluation map}. A translator $T$ is \emph{$\varepsilon$-sound on $\mathcal{C}$
along $u$} if for every $q\in\mathrm{dom}(T)$, every $D\in\mathcal{C}$ and every
$t\in\mathbb{T}$,
\[
d\big(\sem{q}_s(\phi_\rho(D),t),\ \sem{T(q)}_t(D,u(t))\big)\le\varepsilon,
\]
and \emph{exact} on $\mathcal{C}$ along $u$ when $\varepsilon=0$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
The $\phi_\rho$ is the ingestion mapping of \S\ref{s:sec:intro}: the two systems do not
receive the same $D$, so the source query is evaluated on the database the source system
would have been given. Setting $\phi_\rho=\mathrm{id}$ recovers the naive definition, and
the complete paper builds $\phi_\rho$ and proves the results that depend on it.
Everything claimed here is exact, on a class $\mathcal{C}$ the claim states. That is a
deliberate choice of target: an $\varepsilon$-sound translation of a dashboard is a
dashboard whose numbers are nearly right, which is a worse engineering outcome than a
refusal, because nothing downstream can tell.

\begin{definition}[Coverage]\label{s:def:kappa}
Let $Q$ be a finite multiset of queries --- a corpus, with multiplicity, so a query used
on forty dashboards counts forty times. The \emph{coverage} of $T$ on $Q$ is
$\kappa(T,Q)=|\{q\in Q : T(q)\neq\bot\}|/|Q|$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
This counts queries the translator is \emph{willing} to translate, which is the weaker of
two measures: the stronger runs $q$ against the source system and $T(q)$ against the
target on the same data and counts a query only when the two results agree to within
$\varepsilon$. The complete paper runs that differential measure against a real Datadog
account and a local Prometheus, and the two measures agree on every query shape it could
compare. Every $\kappa$ quoted here is Definition~\ref{s:def:kappa}.
