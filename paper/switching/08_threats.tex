\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{s2:sec:threats}

\paragraph{The corpus is published dashboards, and its Datadog half has one source.}
Vendor and community dashboards may be tidier and more heavily templated than in-house
ones. The template-variable share --- $88.6\%$ of Datadog and $82.5\%$ of PromQL
occurrences --- is the figure most likely to be inflated this way, and the $97.0\%$ rollup
dependence is exposed for the same reason: a dashboard that must ship generically has a
weaker reason to pin an interval. $\kappa$ is less exposed, since the gaps it reports are
structural constructs rather than stylistic ones. Worse, all $8{,}770$ Datadog occurrences
come from \texttt{integrations-core} --- one organisation's queries in one house style,
with nothing to pair against the PromQL side's two independent sources, because no
comparable public body of customer-written Datadog dashboards exists.
The paragraph below holds out the community- and partner-maintained sibling repository,
whose house style is measurably different ($77.0\%$ against $88.6\%$), which confirms the
template share as source-specific in the direction this paragraph fears. What neither
repository is, is evidence about what customers write, and that bears on the $491$ most
sharply: the declarations the type inference is checked against come from the same
repository as the queries, so the disagreement is measured between two artefacts by the
same authors. That is a strong signal about an inference which ought to agree with them and
does not, and no evidence at all about how often it fails on the $736$ occurrences naming
metrics this repository never mentions. A corpus of private dashboards would be the better
test and was not obtainable for this work; that is a statement about our access, not about
whether such a corpus could exist.

\paragraph{The grammar and the implementation order were fitted to this corpus.}
Grammar v2 is v1 plus every production the corpus showed was missing, the translator's
feature set is the $25$ features appearing in some query it translates, and
the implementation order behind \S\ref{s2:sec:coverage}'s ceiling is a greedy
maximum-coverage order computed over this corpus. So $\kappa=96.6\%$ is a resubstitution estimate, and the honest reading of it is
``what this corpus asks for, once someone has read it'' rather than ``what a dashboard
estate should expect''. The one decimal place is a property of the arithmetic, not a claim
to that precision out of sample. It bears on \S\ref{s2:sec:baseline} hardest and in the
direction that favours this paper: OpenObserve's translator never saw this corpus, while
this paper's grammar was extended until it parsed it.

Three channels can be read twice and were, which bounds the worry rather than removing it.
On $497$ community dashboards the corpus never listed, grammar v2 parses $99.6\%$ of the
$12{,}522$ distinct strings whose instantiated form the corpus does not hold, against
$99.0\%$ in sample; on $2{,}572$ distinct queries from \texttt{integrations-extras} it
parses $99.9\%$, its in-sample rate; and $\kappa$ itself, frozen and run on $3{,}065$
held-out occurrences, is $96.7\%$ against $96.6\%$ here, where the dashboard-clustered
standard error on the held-out figure is $1.1$ points and rests on an effective two
dashboards' worth of independent variation. Neither parse-rate fall is larger than the
spread it inherits from the two margins behind it. What all three support is that nothing
large is hiding on that side, which is weaker than the rates agreeing and is the claim the
data carries. Nothing is read twice on the other channels: the differential run, the
dual-write replay and the trial probes all run against one account's data and have no
holdout at all.

\paragraph{The differential rig holds true what this paper says the cost is.}
The complete paper runs every distinct translated query shape against a real Datadog
account and a local Prometheus, and all $330$ of the $333$ shapes it could compare agree on
every one of $22{,}692$ bucket comparisons. As a check on $\kappa$ that is worth less than
it looks. Given the kernel soundness theorem, agreement is what the theorem
\emph{predicts}; the run's value is the four translator defects it found on the way there,
against six systematic failures from the same rig, every one of them the harness rather
than the systems. More to the point: the rig satisfies all six of the theorem's hypotheses
by construction, the metric type among them, which it submits under the kind the translator
\emph{inferred} from the query text. The conditions \S\ref{s2:sec:unsaid} argues are the
real switching cost are exactly the ones the rig holds true, so the run is silent on what
happens when they fail. That is still the right design --- a run in which the hypotheses
were already false would measure the hypotheses rather than the arithmetic --- but it means
no experiment here tests the thing this paper concludes.

\paragraph{Everything here is about the query layer, on one ingestion path, in one
direction.}
What we measure is the cost of rewriting saved queries. A real migration also
re-instruments services, re-models metric cardinality and cost, re-establishes retention
and rebuilds alerting, none of which is measured here or follows from anything that is, so
the claim that the dashboard layer is where the cost concentrates is a claim \emph{within}
the query problem rather than a ranking of it against the rest of a migration --- and the
cost of retraining the people who read the dashboards may well dominate both. $\phi_\rho$
models the agent's cumulative-to-delta conversion, while OTLP ingestion applies the same
rule but maps metric temporality, type and datapoint attributes by its own
table~\citep{ddotlp}, so which series exist and what kind each one is are properties of a
configured pipeline rather than of ``Datadog'': a migration on a different path inherits the
framework but not the constants. And $\kappa$ is measured on Datadog metrics into PromQL
only; nothing here extends to logs or traces.

\paragraph{The comparison between the catalogue's columns is not a ratio.}
Weighting by occurrence is the right measure for a migration and the wrong one for a
language comparison, though both are in Table~\ref{s2:tab:coverage} and agree to within a
tenth of a point here. More seriously, comparing what the catalogue's first two columns
hold against the $3.3\%$ of \S\ref{s2:sec:gaps} sets a property of the languages beside a
property of one translator's state on one afternoon: the second falls as work is done and
has already fallen twice during this paper, and the first has moved eight times and holds
$0.2\%$ of the corpus undecided even now. Read as an argument that the main direction's
cost is engineering, that comparison is sound; read as a precise ratio, it is not.
