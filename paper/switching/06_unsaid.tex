\section{What a Dashboard Query Does Not Say}
\label{s2:sec:unsaid}

The gap catalogue is the wrong place to look for the design artefact. The caveats are the
right one. Every translation this paper calls exact is exact relative to stated
preconditions, and Table~\ref{s2:tab:caveats} counts how often each is in play. These are
not hedges added after the fact: each is a diagnostic the emitter attaches to the expression
it returns, in the second component of $E$.

\subsection{A slot the source language does not have}
\label{s2:sec:rollup}

\texttt{ROLLUP\_DEFAULT} covers the $97.0\%$ of translations whose source query never
stated a rollup interval. Datadog's grammar offers \texttt{.rollup($\mathit{op}$,
$\Delta$)} and most queries do not use it, so the interval comes from the dashboard's time
range and no care by the query's author puts it in the text. This is the single largest
qualification on $\kappa$, and it is a property of the source language rather than of the
translator: a Datadog query is not self-contained, so a translated query cannot be either.

PromQL does have the slot --- a range selector is part of the expression --- and that is
the contrast this paper used to draw categorically. It should not be, and the corpus says
so. Of the $3{,}610$ PromQL occurrences carrying a range selector at all, $2{,}006$ ---
$55.6\%$ --- fill it with a dashboard template variable rather than a duration,
overwhelmingly \texttt{\$\_\_rate\_interval} or \texttt{\$\_\_interval}: those panels take
their window from the dashboard's time range too, by a different mechanism and with the
same consequence for anyone translating them. So the comparison is $97.0\%$ against
$55.6\%$ --- a real difference, and one of degree. The categorical version was available to
be checked against this corpus from the beginning and was not, which makes it the one claim
in this paper that went longest without the control it needed.

The exposure runs one way. A dashboard that must ship generically has a weaker reason to
pin a rollup than a hand-tuned production one, so $97.0\%$ is better read as an upper
estimate of what a customer estate would show --- and the conclusion survives the
discount, because the load-bearing $55.6\%$ was measured over two independent PromQL
sources.

\paragraph{What the default costs.}
An exposure is not an error rate, and for some of these occurrences the error rate can be
computed. Joining each translated occurrence to its widget's declared time span, and that
span to a measurement of Datadog's own interval lookup at all ten spans the corpus's
widgets declare, gives this. Of the $8{,}217$ occurrences carrying
\texttt{ROLLUP\_DEFAULT}, $496$ --- $6.0\%$ --- sit in a widget that pins its own span. For
those the emitter's $60$\,s is the interval Datadog would have used for $164$ of them and
the wrong one for $332$, or $66.9\%$. The modal declared span is one hour, $205$
occurrences, where Datadog rolls up at $20$\,s and the emitted \texttt{[60s]} answers a
question three times as wide; the eight spans those widgets declare run from one minute,
where $60$\,s is $60$ times too wide, to one week, where it is a sixtieth of the interval
Datadog uses. The corpus declares two spans no metric query occurrence sits under, and at
the widest of them, a month, the emitted constant is a $240$th of Datadog's interval. All
ten were measured, so none of this rests on interpolation.

The other $7{,}721$ cannot be decided, and the distinction matters more than the ratio
does. A widget that pins nothing takes its span from the dashboard's time selector, which
is a viewer's choice at view time and appears nowhere in the JSON --- so for those the
default is \emph{unverified} rather than wrong. Carrying the measured share across all
$8{,}217$ would give about $5{,}500$, which is an extrapolation and an upper estimate, since
an author who pins a span is doing something deliberate and the widgets that declare are if
anything enriched for disagreement.

What this does not do is move $\kappa$. The $60$\,s is a placeholder for computing
coverage, every occurrence that carries it is counted as translated either way, and the
kernel soundness theorem's hypothesis on the bucket is stated in terms of the width of the
bucket Datadog reports rather than in terms of $60$\,s --- so a translator that reads the
span off the dashboard discharges the hypothesis and emits a different constant, without
changing which queries translate. The figure is the size of the correction a real
per-dashboard translator makes, and it is invisible in every coverage number this paper
prints.

\subsection{A type neither language can state}
\label{s2:sec:type}

\texttt{METRIC\_TYPE\_UNKNOWN} marks $100\%$ of the translated set, and calling that a
second instance of self-containment double-counts a fact this paper has already banked.
Neither language declares a metric's type in the query text: Definition~\ref{s2:def:model}
puts the kind on the series, and choosing \texttt{rate} over \texttt{avg\_over\_time}
records what a PromQL author believed exactly as \texttt{.as\_count()} records what a
Datadog author believed. What makes the type load-bearing \emph{here} is that $\phi_\rho$
branches on it: Datadog stores deltas for a \texttt{COUNT} and samples for a
\texttt{GAUGE}, so one query text reads two different stored quantities where Prometheus
stores samples either way. That is the ingestion mapping showing up a second time, not a
second property of the language, and the ledger should not charge it twice.

Charging it once is enough, because it has a price. The type the query omits is declared,
for $89.5\%$ of translated occurrences, in the repository the dashboards come from, and the
front end's inference is wrong on $6.5\%$ of those. That is $491$ panels a migration would
carry across believing them exact. The $887$ it does not settle divide into $736$ naming
metrics the repository never declares, $134$ rate metrics it declares and this translator
reads as counters, and $17$ belonging to three metric names two integrations declare
incompatibly.

What the repository declares is what an integration \emph{submits}, so a disagreement says
the inference is wrong and not that it must be --- a dashboard can query a metric the
integration does not ship --- which makes the declaration a sharper instrument than the
query text it replaces rather than a proof. $\kappa$ is a coverage measure and never was a
correctness measure, and this is the first number in the paper that says how far apart the
two are.

One piece of syntax looks like the query supplying its own metadata and is not, and
noticing that widened the caveat rather than narrowing it. \texttt{.as\_count()} and
\texttt{.as\_rate()} are documented for \texttt{COUNT} metrics, and we read their presence
as settling the type the dashboard omits, which exempted a quarter of the corpus. Datadog
does not enforce the documentation: on a \texttt{GAUGE} it accepts both modifiers and
ignores them. So the exemption was an artefact of reading a manual instead of an account,
and every translated occurrence now carries the caveat. The language did not become less
self-contained when this was noticed; it never was, and the appearance that a quarter of it
was came from the one piece of syntax resembling a type declaration.

\paragraph{The rest of the table, and one conclusion.}
Four codes remain. \texttt{ROLLUP\_ALIGNMENT} is universal because Datadog's buckets are
epoch-aligned while a PromQL range window follows the evaluation step, so the two systems
agree bucket for bucket on the aligned grid and not off it. \texttt{COUNTER\_RESET} and
\texttt{PHI\_SERIES\_START} each mark the same $23.7\%$ --- the translations using the
cumulative-difference form --- for two conditions that coincide in this corpus without
being the same condition. And \texttt{TAG\_PRESENCE\_EMPTY} marks the $1.4\%$ whose
selector asks a tag merely to be present, where the three tag states Datadog distinguishes
collapse to two in Prometheus's data model: the same barrier that refuses
\texttt{SCOPE\_EMPTY\_VALUE} outright in Table~\ref{s2:tab:gaps}, appearing here as a
condition on the target database rather than as a refusal. The conclusion is the one an
earlier draft of this section had backwards. That draft said $116$ occurrences carried
\texttt{ROLLUP\_ALIGNMENT} alone and so rested on nothing but the evaluation grid; with
\texttt{METRIC\_TYPE\_UNKNOWN} recorded the true figure is \emph{none}, since every one of
those $116$ is an \texttt{avg} rollup, and once the modifiers' evidential value is
withdrawn as well, no translation in this corpus is exact on the strength of anything the
query itself contains. That is a stronger claim than the one it replaces, and it was
invisible only because a precondition was.

\paragraph{Two defaults, and why the counts mislead.}
The type default is wrong for $491$ of the occurrences it can settle and the interval
default for $332$, which look alike --- but the denominators differ by an order of
magnitude. So the default this paper had been quoting is almost always right on a population
that is almost always decidable, and the one it had not computed is wrong two times in three
on a population that is almost never decidable. That is the sharper statement of the
division: the ingestion pillar produces the errors a translator can find and the language
pillar the ones it cannot, since a widget that pins no span has no span to read and nothing
in the query, the response or the repository supplies it. It is not a claim that either
pillar produces more of them.

\subsection{Three more measurements, arrived at from elsewhere}

Three further figures point the same way, and none of the three was collected for this
purpose.

The first is the template-variable share. $88.6\%$ of Datadog query occurrences and
$82.5\%$ of PromQL ones carry at least one template variable; on the held-out Datadog
repository the share is $77.0\%$, so read the level as this corpus's and what generalises
as the fact that both languages carry the dependency. The parse rates of
\S\ref{s2:sec:translator} are the same fact counted as an instrument failure.

The second looks least like the others and is the sharpest. Of $8{,}587$ PromQL regex
matchers in the corpus, $87.9\%$ have a template variable as their pattern. For those the
reverse direction is not hard but \emph{statically undefined}: there is no pattern to
convert until somebody looks at the dashboard. The theorems of \S\ref{s2:sec:reverse} are
about the other eighth.

The third arrives from a competitor, and it is worth a subsection of its own.

\subsection{A baseline, and the same finding from outside}
\label{s2:sec:baseline}

$\kappa$ has nothing to be compared against in the literature, but it does have something
to be compared against in practice. OpenObserve ships a dashboard migrator whose
Datadog-to-PromQL translation runs in the browser, so it can be driven over this corpus
directly; holding aside the $117$ occurrences whose source is several comma-separated
metrics, where our emitter returns a list and theirs one string, leaves $8{,}653$. Their
translator refuses nothing, emitting an expression for every one of them against our
$96.7\%$ --- but $457$, or $5.3\%$, are not PromQL, judged by this paper's grammar, which
predates both translators' output and which Prometheus~3.15.0's own parser agrees with on
every one of the $14{,}555$ distinct expressions the two emit between them. So on the one
measure both tools can be held to without first agreeing on semantics, coverage by
\emph{valid} output, the figures are $94.7\%$ against $96.7\%$: the translator that refuses
more emits more. The comparison is in-sample on one side and out-of-sample on the other, in
the direction that favours this paper, and \S\ref{s2:sec:threats} says so.

Two of their figures are this section's finding arriving from outside it. $1{,}844$
occurrences --- $21.3\%$ --- are given a \texttt{[5m]} window no query states, under the
note ``adjust interval as needed''; and $525$ occurrences have a Datadog modifier silently
dropped, $570$ drops in all, of which $316$ are \texttt{.rollup}, $184$ \texttt{.fill} and
$70$ \texttt{.weighted}. Dropping a \texttt{.rollup} does not approximate the query, it
answers a different one. The interval is not in the query, so a translator must be told it,
record that it guessed, or guess quietly: theirs guesses and mentions it in prose, ours
records \texttt{ROLLUP\_DEFAULT} and counts it. Somebody who was not looking for this
finding ran into it anyway.

One of their three dropped modifiers is dropped correctly --- \texttt{.weighted()} computes
nothing, which this paper established only after calling it inexpressible for $70$
occurrences --- so there their silent drop is right and our earlier refusal was wrong. And
what happens to the queries we refuse is the sharper result: all $275$ of them receive an
expression, across every one of the five gap codes. None of it is concealed, since the
output carries a note, but a note is not a code: $68.6\%$ of occurrences carry only the
cosmetic observation that dots became underscores, so the $31.3\%$ that say something
substantive arrive in the same channel as the noise and cannot be counted without being
read.

\subsection{The lock-in surface is the dashboard layer}
\label{s2:sec:dashboard-layer}

What all of it says is that the object a migration ports is not a query. It is a query
schema plus a resolution layer --- time range, variable bindings, metric metadata --- that
belongs to the dashboard tool and has no counterpart in either language's grammar. In the
terms of \S\ref{s2:sec:background}, the translation needs two attributes and the parse tree
has no production to inherit them from. That relocates the switching cost rather than
dissolving it, and it relocates it somewhere neither $\kappa$ nor a gap catalogue can see,
because a schema parses and translates exactly like a query and is counted as covered.

Two consequences follow and neither is a hedge. For a migration, the work is not where
$\kappa$ points: the $287$ refused occurrences are $262$ distinct strings to rewrite by
hand, while $8{,}217$ translated occurrences need an interval supplied from outside the
query and all $8{,}475$ need a metric type supplied the same way --- and for $496$ of those
intervals the dashboard holds a value to read while for the other $7{,}721$ it does not, so
for most of the estate there is no value anywhere to recover, only a decision to make.
Recovering context is the task; translating the query text is nearly solved.

For the standardisation effort of \citet{sharma2025}, a common query language leaves this
untouched: agreeing on syntax and semantics for metric queries would not make this corpus
portable, because what the corpus is missing is not in the query. That is the one
recommendation this paper is in a position to make to that work, and it is uncomfortable.

\paragraph{One limit, on this and on anyone else's version of it.}
The resolution layer named here is not an abstraction the two query languages share. It is
two products' designs --- Grafana's templating and Datadog's own time selector --- read off
the dashboards this corpus happens to contain, so a portability claim about dashboards is
partly a claim about Grafana. The direction of the finding survives that, since both
products leave the time range and the variable bindings outside the query text and the
metric's type is outside both \emph{languages} whatever tool draws the panel. What does not
survive it is any claim about a third tool, which may place the same three things somewhere
else.
