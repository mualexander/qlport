\section{Coverage}
\label{s2:sec:coverage}

The measure is coverage $\kappa$ of Definition~\ref{s2:def:kappa}, by occurrence rather
than by distinct string, because a widget repeated across forty dashboards is forty panels
a migration has to rebuild by hand if it fails. Queries the grammar cannot parse stay in
the denominator, so $\kappa$ is not reported against the part of the corpus that happened
to cooperate.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\footnotesize
\input{tables/translation_coverage}
\caption{Translation outcomes over the $8{,}770$ Datadog metric query occurrences. Every
$\bot$ carries a gap code and appears in Table~\ref{s2:tab:gaps}; the unparsed row is kept
in the denominator so that $\kappa$ is a statement about the corpus rather than about the
parsable part of it.}
\label{s2:tab:coverage}
\end{table}

\noindent
$\kappa = 96.6\%$ of occurrences and $96.5\%$ of distinct queries. The two agreeing to
within a tenth of a point is worth a sentence, because there was no reason they should: the
queries the translator refuses are neither more nor less repeated across dashboards than
the ones it handles, so there is no cheap win hiding in the popular queries and no
consolation that the failures are all one-offs. Every emitted expression parses under the
PromQL grammar of \S\ref{s2:sec:translator}, checked as an assertion rather than reported
as a rate, since the only acceptable number of syntactically invalid outputs is zero.

\subsection{The gap catalogue}
\label{s2:sec:gaps}

Table~\ref{s2:tab:gaps} is the other half of the answer: not ``what fraction fails'' but
``which constructs, and why''. Every row is reached by a query a real dashboard ships, and
every $\bot$ carries one code.

\caption{The complete gap catalogue for the forward direction. Five codes account for

The division the catalogue was built for is between what the target cannot \emph{hold},
what it cannot \emph{say}, and what we have not \emph{built}. It is still the right division
and the answer it returns is not the one this paper spent most of its life reporting: on
this corpus, in this direction, the first two kinds hold nothing shown to be beyond reach.
All $287$ refused occurrences are unwritten code, with one qualification that carries
everywhere the column is mentioned --- $14$ are refused on a hypothesis this paper can test
for the metrics the vendor declares and cannot test for theirs, so they are undecided rather
than retracted.

\paragraph{Eight corrections, all in one direction.}
Keeping the three kinds apart is what made each departure from the inexpressible column a
finding rather than a revision, and it is also what makes the record embarrassing. Eight
times a refusal or a classification put something in that column that did not belong
there: $348$ occurrences refused on a commutation argument the real evaluation order never
raises; all $124$ disjunctive scopes called a language gap when $120$ were a single regex
matcher away and the four remaining were sayable too; \texttt{.weighted()} called
inexpressible for $70$ occurrences when the modifier computes nothing at all; $36$
zero-fills called inexpressible on the strength of one candidate expression, when a real
server supplies the value per series for another; $31$ occurrences of a wildcard over tag
\emph{keys} filed as beyond the reach of any engineering, when no Datadog query ever asked
for one and the string was manufactured by this paper's own normaliser truncating a
template variable at its first hyphen; $34$ percentile queries refused under a corollary
whose hypothesis provably fails for $20$ of them and is untested for the other $14$; and
$5$ scopes refused for a distinction Prometheus's data model defines away, which is
precisely what makes dropping the term exact rather than lossy.

Together those are $648$ distinct occurrences and $7.4$ points of $\kappa$ --- a running
total across the whole life of the work rather than a figure that stood at any one moment,
and one that still includes the $14$ this paper cannot decide either way. Every one of the
eight erred in the direction that would have made the thesis look better, and the last four
are the least reassuring: the first four surfaced while something else was being measured,
which is luck, and these four came from going looking.

There is a ninth correction and it runs the other way. The formula layer the evaluation
holds aside was held aside partly on the claim that nothing in it computes what a query
cannot, and $30$ occurrences over $19$ distinct strings do: proprietary detectors,
exponentially weighted means, a smoothing window the service rather than the query
chooses, and calendar offsets PromQL's fixed-duration \texttt{offset} cannot express. So
what this section reports is a result about the metric query language and not about
everything a Datadog widget can be made to compute. Eight corrections ran towards the
thesis and the ninth runs away from it, which is weak evidence that the eight were a
property of where the paper was looking rather than of what it was hoping to find --- but
it is the first datum of that kind, and one is not many.

\paragraph{What the ceiling is, and what it costs.}
\texttt{UNSUPPORTED\_FUNCTION} is almost all of what remains, led by \texttt{top(...)} at
$139$ occurrences. That one is expressible: PromQL denotes Datadog's ranking with an
\texttt{@}-pinned \texttt{topk}, read off a real server, and the vendor's own
\texttt{top()} was then measured to behave as its documentation says. Nine more of its
$208$ are a negated match-all, \texttt{\{*,!*\}}, where a live account selects the same
series as for \texttt{\{!*\}}, so the translation is to drop the term. The ceiling is
therefore $99.9\%$ once the unwritten code is written, or $99.7\%$ on the reading that the
$14$ percentile occurrences this paper cannot decide turn out not to be writable.

Reaching it is not proportional work, which the complete paper measures as a staircase
rather than a curve: coverage is conjunctive over the features a query uses while
popularity is marginal, so five of Datadog's $28$ features reach $1.6\%$ of the corpus and
half of them reach $88.7\%$. For anyone reading Table~\ref{s2:tab:gaps} as a work plan, the
order matters more than the list.

That ceiling also carries a condition it usually travels without, and the condition is this
paper's result arriving as a term in its own coverage number. $139$ of the $287$ refusals
are \texttt{top(...)}, whose PromQL form needs the dashboard's time range twice over ---
once as a window and once as an \texttt{@} anchor --- and the query states neither. That the
range is load-bearing rather than cosmetic is itself measured: narrowing the window moves
which series Datadog's own \texttt{top()} returns. So the ceiling is reachable only by a
translator that is told what the query does not say.
