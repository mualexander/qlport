\section{Formal Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

``Are these languages inter-translatable'' is not yet a question. It becomes one once
four things are fixed: what the systems store, what a query denotes on what they store,
when two denotations count as the same, and what a translator is permitted to do when it
cannot proceed. This section fixes them, and the rest of the paper is stated in the
vocabulary it sets up.

\begin{problem}
Let $L_s$ and $L_t$ be query languages over a shared class of time-series databases.
Determine the largest fragment of $L_s$ that admits a meaning-preserving translation into
$L_t$, exhibit the obstructions outside it, and measure what share of queries written in
practice the fragment contains.
\end{problem}

\noindent
Three features of that statement shape everything after it. It asks for a
\emph{fragment}, so the answer is a set of queries and not a yes or no. It asks for the
obstructions, so a translator that fails must say why in a form that can be counted.
And it asks what share of \emph{practice} the fragment covers, which makes the
corpus of \S\ref{sec:evaluation} part of the result rather than an illustration of it.

\subsection{What the systems store}

\begin{definition}[Series, database, result]\label{def:model}
A \emph{series} is a pair $s=(\lambda,\langle (t_1,v_1),\dots,(t_n,v_n)\rangle)$ of a
finite label set $\lambda$ --- a partial map from label names to strings --- and samples
at strictly increasing times $t_i\in\mathbb{T}$ with values $v_i\in\mathbb{R}$. A series
carries a \emph{kind}: $\mathsf{counter}$ for a cumulative total, $\mathsf{delta}$ for a
per-interval increment, $\mathsf{gauge}$ otherwise. A \emph{database} $D$ is a finite set
of series with pairwise distinct label sets, and $\mathcal{D}$ is the class of all
databases. A \emph{result} is a finite partial map from label sets to $\mathbb{R}$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Two choices here are not neutral. Requiring distinct label sets makes a label set the
identity of a series, which is what both systems assume and what lets a result be keyed
by one. And the kind is part of the series, not of the query --- a point
\S\ref{sec:emission} returns to, because a Datadog query does not record it and the
translation depends on it.

\subsection{Queries, denotations, translators}

A query language is a set of strings with a meaning. We take the set to be generated by a
grammar, which is what makes \S\ref{sec:grammars} a question about formal languages
rather than about parsers, and the meaning to be a function of a database and an instant.

\begin{definition}[Query language]\label{def:lang}
A \emph{query language} is a pair $(G,\sem{\cdot})$ of a context-free grammar $G$ and a
denotation $\sem{\cdot}:\Lang(G)\times\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{T}\to\mathit{Result}$.
We write $\Lang(G_{\mathrm{DD}})$ and $\Lang(G_{\mathrm{PQ}})$ for the two languages and
$\sem{\cdot}_{\mathrm{DD}}$, $\sem{\cdot}_{\mathrm{PQ}}$ for their denotations.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Translator]\label{def:translator}
A \emph{translator} is a total function
$T:\Lang(G_s)\to\Lang(G_t)\cup\{\bot\}$, where $\bot$ carries a \emph{gap code} naming
the construct that stopped it. Its \emph{domain} is
$\mathrm{dom}(T)=\{q : T(q)\neq\bot\}$, the fragment of the Problem Statement.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Totality is the substantive requirement. A translator that may crash, or silently emit
something plausible, cannot be measured: the fragment it covers would be whatever it
happened to survive. Requiring every failure to be a $\bot$ with a code makes
$\mathrm{dom}(T)$ observable and makes the complement a \emph{catalogue}
(\S\ref{sec:gaps}) rather than a list of bug reports. \S\ref{sec:lowering} notes the
price: a query is abandoned at its first obstruction, so the codes count first refusals.

\begin{remark}[A refusal is not evidence by itself]\label{rem:refusal}
The discipline has a failure mode this paper walked into and now reports. A gap code
looks like a statement about the languages, and it is in the first instance a statement
about $T$: the construct stopped \emph{this} translator. The two coincide only when
something independent says so --- a proof that no target expression exists
(\S\ref{sec:glob-unions}), or a decision procedure that agrees
(\S\ref{sec:glob-corpus}), or an implementation that removes the refusal and so shows it
was never a gap (\S\ref{sec:gaps}). \S\ref{sec:gaps} and \S\ref{sec:glob-corpus} report
which refusals have which warrant, and the distinction is the single most load-bearing
piece of method here.
\end{remark}

\subsection{When two results agree}

Comparing results needs a distance, not an equality test: the two systems compute in
floating point over different code paths, so bit equality would report disagreement where
none is meant. Relative error is the right scale, because the quantities span many orders
of magnitude and an absolute tolerance would be generous for rates and absurd for counts.

\begin{definition}[Result distance]\label{def:d}
For $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$ put $\delta(a,b)=|a-b|/\max(|a|,|b|)$, with $\delta(0,0)=0$. For
results $r_1,r_2$,
\[
d(r_1,r_2)=\begin{cases}
\max_{\lambda\in\mathrm{dom}(r_1)}\delta\big(r_1(\lambda),r_2(\lambda)\big)
  & \mathrm{dom}(r_1)=\mathrm{dom}(r_2)\\
\infty & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
\end{definition}

\noindent
Differing label sets give $\infty$ rather than a large number because they are a
different kind of error: the translation returned the wrong \emph{series}, and no
tolerance should absorb that.

\begin{lemma}[$d$ is a metric]\label{lem:metric}
$\delta$ is a metric on $\mathbb{R}$, and $d$ is an extended metric on results.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\delta$ is symmetric, and $\delta(a,b)=0$ iff $a=b$. For the triangle inequality, note
$\delta(a,b)\le 2$ always, and $\delta(a,b)\ge 1$ whenever $a$ and $b$ have strictly
opposite signs or one is $0$ and the other is not.

If $a,b,c$ all share a sign, write $r_{xy}=\max(|x|,|y|)/\min(|x|,|y|)\ge 1$ for the ratio
of magnitudes; then $\delta(x,y)=(r_{xy}-1)/r_{xy}$ and $r_{ac}\le r_{ab}\,r_{bc}$. Since
$(r-1)/r$ increases in $r$, it suffices that
$1-\tfrac{1}{st}\le(1-\tfrac1s)+(1-\tfrac1t)$ for $s,t\ge1$, which rearranges to
$(s-1)(t-1)\ge 0$.

If $b$ has the sign opposite to $a$ and $c$, both right-hand terms are at least $1$ and
the left is at most $2$. If $a$ and $c$ have opposite signs, take $|a|\ge|c|$ by symmetry,
so $\delta(a,c)=1+|c|/|a|$, and $b$ shares a sign with one of them, say $a$. When
$|b|\ge|c|$ the claim reduces to
$|c|\big(\tfrac{1}{|a|}-\tfrac{1}{|b|}\big)\le\delta(a,b)$, which holds because
$|c|\le|a|$; when $|b|<|c|$ the right-hand side is
$1-|b|/|a|+|b|/|c|\ge 1\ge\delta(a,c)-|c|/|a|$. Taking a maximum over a common domain
preserves all three properties, and $\infty$ is absorbing.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Lemma~\ref{lem:metric} is not decoration: the admissibility argument of
Proposition~\ref{prop:admissible} is a triangle-inequality argument, and without the
lemma it would be an appeal to intuition about ``closeness''.

\subsection{Exactness, soundness, coverage}

\begin{definition}[Exact and $\varepsilon$-sound]\label{def:sound}
Let $\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{D}$. A translator $T$ is \emph{$\varepsilon$-sound on
$\mathcal{C}$} if for every $q\in\mathrm{dom}(T)$, every $D\in\mathcal{C}$ and every
$t\in\mathbb{T}$,
\[
d\big(\sem{q}_s(D,t),\ \sem{T(q)}_t(D,t)\big)\le\varepsilon,
\]
and \emph{exact} on $\mathcal{C}$ when $\varepsilon=0$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
\S\ref{sec:ingestion} replaces $\sem{q}_s(D,t)$ here by
$\sem{q}_s(\phi_\rho(D),t)$, because the two systems do not receive the same $D$;
Definition~\ref{def:phi-sound} is that generalisation and Definition~\ref{def:sound} is
its special case $\phi_\rho=\mathrm{id}$. Everything this paper claims is exact in the
sense above, on a class $\mathcal{C}$ that the claim states. That is a deliberate choice
of target: an $\varepsilon$-sound translation of a dashboard is a dashboard whose numbers
are nearly right, which is a worse engineering outcome than a refusal, because nothing
downstream can tell.

\begin{definition}[Coverage]\label{def:kappa}
Let $Q$ be a finite multiset of queries --- a corpus, with multiplicity, so a query used
on forty dashboards counts forty times. The \emph{coverage} of $T$ on $Q$ is
\[
\kappa(T,Q)=\frac{|\{q\in Q : T(q)\neq\bot\}|}{|Q|}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Two coverages, and which one this paper reports]\label{rem:two-kappas}
Definition~\ref{def:kappa} counts queries the translator is \emph{willing} to translate.
The stronger measure counts queries whose translation is observed to agree: run $q$
against the source system and $T(q)$ against the target on the same data, and count a
query only when the two results agree to within $\varepsilon$. That is the differential
measure, and it requires both systems live. \S\ref{sec:differential} runs it over every
distinct query shape among the occurrences this translator emits a single expression
for, against a real Datadog account and a local Prometheus; the two measures agree on
every shape it could compare. The two coincide
exactly when every emitted expression is correct, and the four translator defects that
run found are what the gap between them consisted of before it was closed --- which is
also why the exactness proofs of \S\ref{sec:kernel-soundness} are kept rather than
replaced by the measurement: a run on synthetic series over one account is evidence, and
a proof on a stated class is a reason. Every $\kappa$ quoted below is
Definition~\ref{def:kappa}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The three questions}

\begin{description}
\item[RQ1 (\S\ref{sec:grammars})] Do these languages admit deterministic parsers with
one token of lookahead --- are they LALR(1)? A positive answer gives each query a unique
parse tree, which is what makes a syntax-directed translation well defined at all.
\item[RQ2 (\S\ref{sec:translation}, \S\ref{sec:regular})] What is $\mathrm{dom}(T)$ for
the largest exact translator we can build, and what are the obstructions outside it? The
Problem Statement's fragment, with the gap catalogue as the second half of the answer.
\item[RQ3 (\S\ref{sec:evaluation})] What is $\kappa(T,Q)$ on a corpus of dashboards
people actually publish, and how is the shortfall distributed over language features?
\end{description}

\noindent
The three are not independent, and the dependence runs one way: RQ1 licenses the
syntax-directed translation RQ2 builds, and RQ2's domain is what RQ3 measures. A negative
answer to RQ1 would not have stopped the paper --- an ambiguous grammar can still be
parsed --- but it would have made every later claim conditional on a disambiguation
policy, which is why it comes first.
