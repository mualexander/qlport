\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

Four traditions bear on the question of \S\ref{sec:intro} and none of them answers it.
Formal-language theory supplies the frame; compiler correctness supplies the standard of
proof; the expressiveness literature supplies the notion of ``cannot say''; and the
economics of switching costs supplies the reason anybody should care. What is missing is
the combination, applied to two languages whose systems do not store the same data.

\subsection{Query languages as formal languages}

Rich's §48.1 is the licence for the approach: a query language is a formal language, so
grammars, automata and decidability apply to it~\citep{rich2008}, and which class a
language falls in decides what can be settled mechanically. An unambiguous grammar gives
each query exactly one parse tree, which is the precondition for a translation being
well defined; an LALR(1) one makes the parse linear and the parser a table, so a corpus
can be processed in bulk rather than sampled~\citep{aho2006}.

The translation itself has a classical form. A syntax-directed transduction associates an
output with each production and composes them over the parse tree~\citep{lewis1968}, and
where a node's output depends on information held elsewhere, Knuth's attribute grammars
are the standard account~\citep{knuth1968}. \S\ref{sec:lowering} needs both, because two
attributes are not in the Datadog query text at all. Regular languages then enter
directly: both languages filter tag values with a pattern language --- a glob in one, a
regular expression in the other --- so whether one pattern has a counterpart is a
question about two classes of regular languages, and it is where the sharpest negative
results live (\S\ref{sec:regular}).

\subsection{The two languages}

PromQL is documented and its reference parser is open source, so a grammar can be checked
against an implementation rather than inferred~\citep{promql}. It is a vector-valued
expression language: selectors denote sets of series, range selectors denote windows,
functions reduce windows, and aggregation operators reduce across series. Counters are
stored as running totals and reset handling lives inside the range functions ---
\texttt{rate} and \texttt{increase} are reset-aware, and a recent proposal adds an
\texttt{anchored} modifier that removes their extrapolation~\citep{prom0052}.

Datadog's metric query language is not documented as a grammar. What is published is a
reference manual with examples, enough to infer the syntax and not to verify it
(\S\ref{sec:datadog-inference}). The important difference is not syntactic: Datadog
stores a counter as per-interval deltas, and a query is evaluated first by time and then
by space~\citep{datadog,ddnested} --- a rollup reduces each series alone and a space
aggregator combines the results. Neither the rollup interval nor the metric's type is
part of the query text, which turns out to matter more than any construct either
language has (\S\ref{sec:emission}). Ingest conventions differ again by path: Datadog
documents a backfill window~\citep{ddhistorical} and OTLP maps types by its own
table~\citep{ddotlp}, which is why \S\ref{sec:ingestion} parameterises the mapping
rather than describing ``Datadog''.

\subsection{What a translation guarantees}

SQL dialect translation is the closest established practice. Apache Calcite parses SQL
into a relational algebra, optimises, and generates dialect-specific SQL on the way
out~\citep{begoli2018}; SQLGlot does the same across more than twenty
dialects~\citep{sqlglot}. Both are engineering successes and neither offers a statement
of what a translation preserves: their criterion is that the output parses and behaves as
users expect, established by test suites, and where a construct has no counterpart the
usual outcome is a best-effort rewrite that leaves no record of the approximation. That
is a reasonable engineering choice and a poor measurement instrument, which is why
\S\ref{sec:framework} requires a translator to be total with every failure a named
$\bot$.

Converters for this language pair in particular exist, and we have found no published
result among them --- a negative about a literature, which is the weakest kind of claim
this paper makes and is stated as a search rather than as a fact. Chronosphere describes the closest thing to \S\ref{sec:translation}'s method ---
a Datadog grammar written from scratch, queries parsed to an AST, and that AST emitted
through the open-source Prometheus grammar --- and reports getting ``about 90\%'' of the
way, the remainder left to hand~\citep{marcopoli2024}; what the percentage is a percentage
of, and what a converted query is meant to preserve, are not stated. OpenObserve's
dashboard migrator translates metric panels and metric monitors into PromQL and marks each
object migrated, needing review, or skipped~\citep{openobserve} --- a coarse form of the
typed failure \S\ref{sec:framework} requires, reported without counts. Neither of these is
a criticism of the tools: a migration tool is judged by whether the dashboard comes up.
It does mean the quantities this paper reports --- what is impossible rather than
unbuilt, how often a translation rests on something the query does not say, and how
often the resulting guess is wrong --- have no \emph{published} measurement to be set
against. They are not beyond comparison, though: OpenObserve's translator turns out to
run in the browser, so \S\ref{sec:baseline} drives it over this corpus and asks the one
question both tools can be held to without first agreeing on semantics --- what each
emits, and whether what it emits is PromQL.

The standard of proof sits in compiler verification. McCarthy and Painter proved a
compiler for arithmetic expressions correct by relating source and target
denotations~\citep{mccarthy1967}, and CompCert carries the idea through a realistic C
compiler with machine-checked proofs~\citep{leroy2009}. That is the shape of
\S\ref{sec:kernel-soundness}'s theorem though not its rigour --- the proof here is on
paper and for a fragment --- and it differs in one way that is not incidental: a verified
compiler may assume source and target run on the same input, and here they do not, which
is what \S\ref{sec:ingestion} exists to handle. Database theory supplies the adjacent
decision problem, equivalence and containment of conjunctive queries being decidable by
the homomorphism characterisation~\citep{chandra1977,abiteboul1995}; it does not transfer,
since the quantities here are numeric aggregates over time windows and exactness has to
be stated up to floating point. The borrowing is the habit: state the equivalence you
mean, then decide it.

``PromQL cannot say this'' is also a claim with a literature behind it. Felleisen
formalises expressive power by asking whether a construct can be eliminated by a local,
meaning-preserving rewriting~\citep{felleisen1991}, and Mitchell relates expressiveness
to observational equivalence~\citep{mitchell1993}. What this paper borrows is the
\emph{distinction} rather than the criterion: \S\ref{sec:regular}'s results are of a
blunter kind, containment facts about two classes of regular languages where the target
has no term with the denotation at all. They are the only claims here that hold
regardless of how much engineering is applied --- no glob denotes the non-empty tag
values, so no work on a Datadog back end will translate \texttt{.+} into one --- and
separating them from unfinished work is what \S\ref{sec:gaps} measures.

\subsection{Observability, standardisation, and why any of this is a cost}

The closest observability work compares implementations of \emph{one} language rather
than translating between two. Volz's compliance testing found that vendors advertising
PromQL support disagree with Prometheus on a generated conformance suite~\citep{volz2020}
--- this paper's problem in miniature, and a warning: if one language's implementations
already differ, a translation between two needs its soundness stated rather than assumed.
The practice of verifying a candidate against a real server rather than a model
(\S\ref{sec:ingestion}) is taken from that result.

Standardisation approaches it from the other end. OpenTelemetry has largely settled the
\emph{data} question~\citep{otel}, leaving queries as the remaining lock-in surface, and
the CNCF's Observability TAG has an active effort towards a common query
language~\citep{sharma2025} --- which needs exactly the evidence this paper produces:
which constructs are genuinely hard to translate and which merely have not been. Among
existing languages LogQL is PromQL-shaped with a log-selection stage in
front~\citep{logql}, while Splunk's SPL is a piped search language whose stages carry
state~\citep{splunk}; \S\ref{sec:discussion} argues the dividing line for this method is
whether a language denotes a function of a database at an instant.

Switching costs themselves are well developed in industrial organisation. Farrell and
Klemperer survey lock-in and its competitive effects~\citep{farrell2007}, and Shapiro and
Varian give the lock-in cycle its standard informal treatment for information
goods~\citep{shapiro1999}. The structural point matters more here than any quantity: what
retains a customer is not that leaving is impossible but that the terms of return differ
from the terms of entry, so a decision made cheaply cannot be unmade cheaply. That is an
asymmetry claim and it is testable, which is what \S\ref{sec:discussion} does with the
two directions. The concrete case that motivates the question is on the public
record~\citep{orosz2023}.

\subsection{What this paper adds}

Each tradition leaves the same thing open. The transpilers translate without stating what
is preserved, and the migration tools for this pair report neither what they preserve nor
what they refuse; the verification work states what is preserved but assumes a shared input;
the expressiveness literature gives the form of a negative result but is rarely pointed
at query languages; the observability work compares implementations of one language, and
the standardisation work needs evidence about which differences are essential; the
economics predicts an asymmetry without measuring one. This paper's contribution is the
combination: soundness stated relative to an ingestion mapping, so that two
differently-stored systems can be compared at all; a translator whose refusals are typed
and counted rather than papered over; non-expressibility proved where it holds; and all
of it measured on a corpus of queries nobody wrote for the purpose.
