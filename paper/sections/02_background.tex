\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

Four traditions bear on the question of \S\ref{sec:intro}, and none of them answers it.
Formal-language theory supplies the frame; compiler correctness supplies the standard of
proof; the expressiveness literature supplies the notion of ``cannot say''; and the
economics of switching costs supplies the reason anybody should care. What is missing is
the combination, applied to two languages whose systems do not store the same data.

\subsection{Query languages as formal languages}

Rich's §48.1 is the licence for the whole approach: a query language is a formal language,
so the machinery of grammars, automata and decidability applies to
it~\citep{rich2008}. Which class a language falls in decides what can be settled about it
mechanically. If a language is context-free it has a grammar; if that grammar is
unambiguous, each query has exactly one parse tree, which is the precondition for any
claim that a translation is \emph{well defined}; and if the grammar is LALR(1) in
particular, the parse is linear and the parser is a table, so a corpus can be processed in
bulk rather than sampled~\citep{aho2006}.

The translation itself has a classical form. A syntax-directed transduction associates an
output with each production and composes them over the parse
tree~\citep{lewis1968}, and where a node's output depends on information held elsewhere
in the tree, Knuth's attribute grammars are the standard
account~\citep{knuth1968}. \S\ref{sec:lowering} needs both: lowering is bottom-up except
for two attributes that the Datadog query text does not carry at all, one of which has to
be synthesised at the query node and inherited downward.

Regular languages enter twice. Both languages filter on tag values with a pattern
language --- a glob in one, a regular expression in the other --- and the question of
whether one pattern has a counterpart in the other is a question about two classes of
regular languages, settled by the equivalence decision procedure
(\S\ref{sec:regular}). This is the part of the problem where the classical theory applies
most directly and, as it turns out, where the sharpest negative results live.

\subsection{The two languages}

PromQL is documented and its reference parser is open source, so a grammar can be checked
against an implementation rather than inferred~\citep{promql}. It is a vector-valued
expression language: selectors denote sets of series, range selectors denote windows,
functions reduce windows, and aggregation operators reduce across series. Counters are
stored as running totals, and reset handling lives inside the range functions ---
\texttt{rate} and \texttt{increase} are reset-aware, and a recent proposal adds an
\texttt{anchored} modifier that removes their extrapolation~\citep{prom0052}.

Datadog's metric query language is not documented as a grammar. What is published is a
reference manual with examples, which is enough to infer the syntax but not to verify it,
and \S\ref{sec:datadog-inference} says what we did instead. Semantically the important
difference is not syntactic at all: Datadog stores a counter as per-interval deltas, and
a query is evaluated ``first by time, then by space'' --- a rollup reduces each series
alone and a space aggregator combines the results~\citep{datadog}. Neither the rollup
interval nor the metric's type is part of the query text, which turns out to matter more
than any construct either language has (\S\ref{sec:emission}). Datadog's own ingest
documents the conversion and the backfill window~\citep{ddhistorical}, and OTLP is a third
ingestion path with different conventions again~\citep{ddotlp}, which is why
\S\ref{sec:ingestion} parameterises the mapping rather than describing ``Datadog''.

\subsection{Translating between dialects, and what that guarantees}

SQL dialect translation is the closest established practice. Apache Calcite parses SQL
into a relational algebra, optimises, and generates dialect-specific SQL on the way out,
which is an IR-based translator of exactly the shape used
here~\citep{begoli2018}. SQLGlot does the same job as a library across more than twenty
dialects~\citep{sqlglot}. Both are engineering successes and neither offers what this
paper needs: a statement of what a translation preserves. Their correctness criterion is
that the output parses and behaves as users expect, established by test suites; when a
construct has no counterpart, the usual outcome is a best-effort rewrite rather than a
refusal, and nothing in the output records that an approximation happened. This is a
reasonable engineering choice and a poor measurement instrument, which is why
\S\ref{sec:framework} requires a translator to be total with every failure a named $\bot$.

The standard of proof sits in compiler verification. McCarthy and Painter proved a
compiler for arithmetic expressions correct by relating the source and target
denotations~\citep{mccarthy1967}, and CompCert carries the same idea through a realistic C
compiler with machine-checked proofs~\citep{leroy2009}. That is the shape of
\S\ref{sec:kernel-soundness}'s theorem, though not its rigour: the proof here is on paper
and for a fragment, and the data model it relates is two \emph{different} stored forms
rather than one. The difference is not incidental. A verified compiler may assume the
source and target run on the same input; here they do not, which is what
\S\ref{sec:ingestion} exists to handle.

Query equivalence is also a studied problem in databases. Containment and equivalence of
conjunctive queries are decidable, by the homomorphism characterisation of Chandra and
Merlin~\citep{chandra1977}, and the surrounding theory is
standard~\citep{abiteboul1995}. It does not transfer directly, because the quantities here
are numeric aggregates over time windows rather than sets of tuples, and because
exactness has to be stated up to floating point --- hence the metric of
\S\ref{sec:framework} rather than an equality test. The relevant borrowing is the habit:
state the equivalence you mean, then decide it.

\subsection{Expressiveness as a formal question}

``PromQL cannot say this'' is a claim with a literature behind it. Felleisen formalises
expressive power by asking whether a construct can be eliminated from a language by a
local, meaning-preserving rewriting~\citep{felleisen1991}, and Mitchell relates
expressiveness to observational equivalence and
abstraction~\citep{mitchell1993}. The results of \S\ref{sec:regular} are
non-expressibility results of this kind, and they are the only claims here that hold
regardless of how much engineering is applied: no glob denotes the non-empty tag values,
so no amount of work on a Datadog back end will translate \texttt{.+} into one. Distinguishing such claims from unfinished work is the discipline
\S\ref{sec:intro} describes and \S\ref{sec:gaps} measures, and the literature is what
makes the distinction sharp rather than rhetorical.

\subsection{Observability-specific work}

The closest observability work compares implementations of \emph{one} language rather than
translating between two. Volz's compliance testing found that vendors advertising PromQL
support disagree with Prometheus on real queries~\citep{volz2020} --- which is this
paper's problem in miniature, and a warning: if one language's implementations already
differ, a translation between two languages needs its soundness stated rather than
assumed. The practice of verifying a candidate against a real server rather than a model
(\S\ref{sec:ingestion}) is taken from that result.

Standardisation efforts approach the problem from the other end. OpenTelemetry has
largely settled the \emph{data} question, so metrics and traces now have a vendor-neutral
wire format~\citep{otel}, leaving queries as the remaining lock-in surface --- and the
CNCF's Observability TAG has an active effort towards a common query
language~\citep{sharma2025}. Where that effort needs evidence is exactly here: which
constructs are genuinely hard to translate, and which merely have not been. Among existing
languages, LogQL is PromQL-shaped with a log-selection stage in
front~\citep{logql}, while Splunk's SPL is a piped search language whose stages carry
state~\citep{splunk}; \S\ref{sec:discussion} argues the dividing line for this method is
whether a language denotes a function of a database at an instant.

\subsection{Why any of this is a cost}

Switching costs are a well-developed subject in industrial organisation. Farrell and
Klemperer survey lock-in and the competitive effects of switching
costs~\citep{farrell2007}, and Shapiro and Varian give the lock-in cycle its standard
informal treatment for information goods~\citep{shapiro1999}. The relevant point for this
paper is structural rather than quantitative: what retains a customer is not that leaving
is impossible but that the terms of return differ from the terms of entry, so a decision
made cheaply cannot be unmade cheaply. That is an asymmetry claim, and it is testable ---
which is what \S\ref{sec:discussion} does with the two directions. The concrete case that
motivates the question is on the public record~\citep{orosz2023}.

\subsection{What this paper adds}

Each tradition above leaves the same thing open. The transpilers translate without
stating what is preserved. The verification work states what is preserved but assumes a
shared input. The expressiveness literature gives the form of a negative result but is
rarely pointed at query languages. The observability work compares implementations of one
language, and the standardisation work needs evidence about which differences are
essential. The economics predicts an asymmetry without measuring one.

This paper's contribution is the combination: soundness stated relative to an ingestion
mapping so that two differently-stored systems can be compared at all; a translator whose
refusals are typed and counted rather than papered over; non-expressibility proved where
it holds; and all of it measured on a corpus of queries nobody wrote for the purpose.
