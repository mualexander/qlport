\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An observability bill is one of the few software costs that can grow faster than the
system it measures, and the published cases are startling: one company's monitoring
invoice reached \$65M a year, run up, by the account its own engineers give, because
nobody was watching infrastructure spend~\citep{orosz2023}. The obvious response is to move to something cheaper, and for
metrics the cheap option is well established --- Prometheus is free, widely deployed, and
its query language is a de facto standard. Teams nonetheless stay, and when they explain
why, the reason is rarely the data. It is the dashboards.

A mature observability estate is thousands of saved queries: panels, alerting rules,
service-level objectives, runbook links. Moving vendors means rewriting all of them in
another language, by hand, without a way to tell whether a rewrite preserved what the
original meant. That is a switching cost, and the interesting question is where it comes
from. If it is incidental --- different keywords, different punctuation --- then a
translator removes it and the lock-in was never real. If it is a consequence of how the
two languages are \emph{designed}, then no amount of engineering removes it, and the
query language is a retention mechanism whether or not anyone intended it as one.

This paper asks which it is, and answers with measurement rather than assertion. The
question is tractable because query languages are formal languages: Rich's §48.1 treats
them as such, and the classification it offers --- regular, context-free, decidable ---
is the frame this paper works in~\citep{rich2008}. If the two languages are context-free,
they have grammars; if their grammars are unambiguous, each query has one parse tree; if
each construct of one has a meaning-preserving counterpart in the other, a translator
exists; and where a construct does not, the obstruction can be exhibited as a theorem
instead of reported as a limitation.

\paragraph{Scope.}
We study \emph{forward portability}: once a team dual-writes or switches, a translated
query must mean the same thing as the original on the data each system receives from then
on. Migrating stored history is a different problem and is out of scope. The main
direction is Datadog metric queries into PromQL, because that is the direction teams
actually take; the reverse direction appears wherever it is cheap to measure and in
\S\ref{sec:regular} in full, because the asymmetry between the two turns out to be the
result.

That asymmetry starts with something easy to miss: the two systems do not store the same
thing. Prometheus keeps a counter as a running total; Datadog keeps it as per-interval
deltas. So a translation is not a function from queries to queries that preserves
meaning on a shared database --- there is no shared database. \S\ref{sec:ingestion}
makes this precise as an ingestion mapping $\phi$ and states soundness relative to it,
which is what lets the paper say that a PromQL query reading absolute counter values has
no Datadog translation \emph{at all}, for reasons of information rather than syntax.

\paragraph{What we did.}
We inferred an LALR(1) grammar for each language and checked it for conflicts; defined an
intermediate representation with a denotation; built a translator that lowers Datadog
queries into it and emits PromQL, refusing with a named gap code rather than guessing;
proved the translations exact under stated preconditions and verified the PromQL side
against a real Prometheus~3.15.0 rather than against our own model of it; and ran the
whole thing over the $8{,}766$ Datadog metric queries of a corpus of $28{,}490$
extracted from $763$ published dashboard and alerting documents, with nothing sampled
and nothing hand-classified.

\paragraph{What we found.}
The thesis survives, but not in the form it was posed. There is a language-design
artefact in the direction teams travel, and it is not an expressiveness gap; the
expressiveness gap is in the direction they do not. Both halves took measurement to
establish and neither was the expected answer.

Going from Datadog to PromQL, $96.3\%$ of query occurrences translate, and the shortfall
is mostly unfinished work rather than impossibility: $1.2\%$ of the corpus is beyond
reach --- two constructs PromQL cannot say and one the target system does not hold ---
against $2.4\%$ this translator has not implemented, with a
ceiling of $98.8\%$ once the latter is done. On that evidence the switching cost in the
direction people travel is \emph{engineering} (\S\ref{sec:gaps}).

The design artefact is elsewhere, and \S\ref{sec:discussion} argues it is the more
interesting finding. A Datadog query does not state its own rollup interval --- that comes
from the dashboard's time range --- and does not state whether its metric is a counter or
a gauge. So $96.9\%$ of the translations we emit depend on an interval the query omits
and \emph{every one} depends on a type it omits. The
translations are exact, but exact relative to context supplied from outside the query. A
PromQL expression names its own window and carries its reset semantics in the function it
calls. That is a property of the languages, and it is invisible in any coverage number.

Coming back is a different matter, and here the obstructions are proved. A Datadog tag
filter is a glob, and \S\ref{sec:regular} shows a glob's language is either a single word
or unbounded in length --- from which it follows that no glob denotes a single arbitrary
character, nor the non-empty values, nor a wildcard confined to part of the alphabet.
Those three cover every refusal in that section, $38.3\%$ of the corpus's matcher
occurrences whose pattern is concrete. Together with the directional barrier of \S\ref{sec:ingestion}, the
picture is an asymmetry: leaving costs engineering, returning costs expressiveness.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Grammars (\S\ref{sec:grammars}).} LALR(1) grammars for both languages,
including one for Datadog's metric query language, which the vendor does not publish and
which had to be inferred from documentation and $8{,}766$ vendor-authored queries. The
Datadog grammar is conflict-free; PromQL has exactly one shift/reduce conflict, and we
show it is forced by the language rather than by our rules.
\item \textbf{$\phi$-relative soundness (\S\ref{sec:ingestion}).} Soundness stated
relative to an ingestion mapping, with the consequence that the information barrier
between these two systems is \emph{directional} --- a result about the languages that
holds for every possible translator, not just ours.
\item \textbf{Exact rollup translations (\S\ref{sec:ingestion}, \S\ref{sec:translation}).}
Datadog's \texttt{.as\_count()} and \texttt{.as\_rate()} translated exactly, not merely
to within $\varepsilon$, with the preconditions named; and soundness theorems whose
hypotheses are the preconditions the emitter reports, plus two it cannot see and the
paper therefore states instead, covering first the kernel
($95.4\%$ of the corpus) and then its arithmetic closure --- which is every expression
this translator emits.
\item \textbf{The regular sub-problem (\S\ref{sec:regular}).} Glob-expressibility of a
regular expression is decidable, by an argument about shortest words, and the
impossibility results above. The construction that makes most of it work is one fact used
in both directions.
\item \textbf{A gap catalogue, split (\S\ref{sec:gaps}).} Every refusal carries a code,
and the catalogue separates three things most translation work runs together: what the
target system does not \emph{hold}, what PromQL cannot \emph{say}, and what we have not
\emph{built}. The first was the surprise: \S\ref{sec:ingestion} argues the information
barrier runs the other way, and one Datadog construct turns out to sit on the wrong side
of it.
\item \textbf{Coverage on real dashboards (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}).} $\kappa$ over the
whole corpus, by occurrence rather than by distinct string, with the implementation order
that produced it and the features that cost the remainder.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{A note on method.}
One discipline runs through all of it and is worth stating in advance, because this paper
got it wrong four times. A translator's refusal looks like a fact about the languages and
is in the first instance a fact about the translator. Separating the two takes something
independent: a proof that no target expression exists, a decision procedure that agrees,
or an implementation that removes the refusal and so demonstrates it was never a gap.
An earlier version of this work refused $348$ query occurrences on a commutation argument
that the real evaluation order never raises; a later one reported all $124$
disjunctive scopes as a language gap when $120$ of them were a single regex matcher away;
a third called \texttt{.weighted()} inexpressible for $70$ occurrences when the modifier
turns out to compute nothing at all; and a fourth counted the four disjunctions no
single key covers as beyond PromQL two paragraphs from the sentence explaining how
PromQL says them. All four
would have produced a stronger-sounding claim, and all four erred in the same direction.
\S\ref{sec:gaps} and \S\ref{sec:glob-corpus} report which refusals now have which
warrant.
