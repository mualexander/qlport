\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An observability bill is one of the few software costs that can grow faster than the
system it measures, and the published cases are startling: one company's monitoring
invoice reached \$65M a year, run up, by the account its own engineers give, because
nobody was watching infrastructure spend~\citep{orosz2023}. The obvious response is to move to something cheaper, and for
metrics the cheap option is well established --- Prometheus is free, widely deployed, and
its query language is a de facto standard. Teams nonetheless stay, and when they explain
why, the reason is rarely the data. It is the dashboards.

A mature observability estate is thousands of saved queries: panels, alerting rules,
service-level objectives, runbook links. Moving vendors means rewriting all of them in
another language, by hand, without a way to tell whether a rewrite preserved what the
original meant. That is a switching cost, and the interesting question is where it comes
from. If it is incidental --- different keywords, different punctuation --- then a
translator removes it and the lock-in was never real. If it is a consequence of how the
two languages are \emph{designed}, then no amount of engineering removes it, and the
query language is a retention mechanism whether or not anyone intended it as one.

This paper asks which it is, and answers with measurement rather than assertion. The
question is tractable because query languages are formal languages: Rich's §48.1 treats
them as such, and the classification it offers --- regular, context-free, decidable ---
is the frame this paper works in~\citep{rich2008}. If the two languages are context-free,
they have grammars; if their grammars are unambiguous, each query has one parse tree; if
each construct of one has a meaning-preserving counterpart in the other, a translator
exists; and where a construct does not, the obstruction can be exhibited as a theorem
instead of reported as a limitation.

\paragraph{Scope.}
We study \emph{forward portability}: once a team dual-writes or switches, a translated
query must mean the same thing as the original on the data each system receives from then
on. Migrating stored history is a different problem and is out of scope. The main
direction is Datadog metric queries into PromQL, because that is the direction teams
actually take; the reverse direction appears wherever it is cheap to measure and in
\S\ref{sec:regular} in full, because the asymmetry between the two turns out to be the
result.

That asymmetry starts with something easy to miss: the two systems do not store the same
thing. Prometheus keeps a counter as a running total; Datadog keeps it as per-interval
deltas. So a translation is not a function from queries to queries that preserves
meaning on a shared database --- there is no shared database. \S\ref{sec:ingestion}
makes this precise as an ingestion mapping $\phi$ and states soundness relative to it,
which is what lets the paper say that a PromQL query reading absolute counter values has
no Datadog translation \emph{at all}, for reasons of information rather than syntax.

\paragraph{What we did.}
We inferred a grammar for each language and checked each for conflicts, finding
Datadog's conflict-free and PromQL's LALR(1) modulo one documented resolution; defined an
intermediate representation with a denotation; built a translator that lowers Datadog
queries into it and emits PromQL, refusing with a named gap code rather than guessing;
proved the translations exact under stated preconditions and verified the PromQL side
against a real Prometheus~3.15.0 rather than against our own model of it; and ran the
whole thing over the $8{,}770$ Datadog metric queries of a corpus of $22{,}894$
extracted from the published dashboard and alerting documents of
Table~\ref{tab:corpus-sources}, with nothing sampled
and nothing hand-classified.

\paragraph{What we found.}
The thesis survives, but not in the form it was posed, and the finding that matters is
not the one the questions above were built to get.

A dashboard query does not determine its own meaning. A Datadog query's slot for the
rollup interval is optional, and $97.0\%$ of the translations we emit are of queries
that leave it empty, so the interval arrives from the dashboard's time range instead.
PromQL's slot is obligatory where it applies, and $55.6\%$ of the corpus's range
selectors fill it with a dashboard variable anyway --- so the contrast between the two
languages is one of degree, and what they share is that the value comes from a layer
outside either of them. On the Datadog side $88.6\%$ of query occurrences carry at least
one template variable and on the PromQL side $82.5\%$ do, which is the measurement that
stops this being a complaint about one vendor. The metric's type is a second thing no
query states and is not a second instance of the same property:
\S\ref{sec:dashboard-layer} is careful that what makes it bite here is the ingestion
mapping branching on it rather than anything either language can or cannot say. The
object a migration ports is therefore a query schema plus a resolution layer belonging
to the dashboard tool, which is where the switching cost we measured actually sits, and
where a common query language would not reach.

Every instrument in this paper is blind to that. A query that does not fix its own
meaning parses like any other, translates like any other, and is counted as covered like
any other, so neither the grammars nor the gap catalogue nor $\kappa$ can see it. That
is why it took all of them to find it, and why the coverage result comes second here
although it came first in the work.

Going from Datadog to PromQL, $96.6\%$ of query occurrences translate, and the shortfall
is \emph{all} unfinished work bar $14$ occurrences: no query in the corpus is shown to be beyond reach. The $3.3\%$
carrying a gap code is code this translator has not written and the $0.1\%$ beside it
does not parse --- $8$ occurrences of one undocumented construct, which this paper
declines to classify, so the ceiling is $99.9\%$ once the first is written with those $8$
left undecided. Both numbers are measured on the corpus the grammar and the
implementation order were fitted to, which \S\ref{sec:threats} states as the threat it
is: nothing here is held out. Reaching an empty column took eight corrections, six of which emptied a row of the
gap catalogue, so
\S\ref{sec:discussion} treats the zero as the least certain number here rather than the
most. That ceiling carries a condition it usually travels without: $139$ of
those $287$ occurrences are \texttt{top(...)}, whose PromQL form needs the dashboard's
time range twice over, so the ceiling is reachable only by a translator that is told
what the query does not say --- the first finding reappearing as a term in the second.
On that evidence the switching cost in the direction people travel is
\emph{engineering} (\S\ref{sec:gaps}).

Translating is not the same as translating correctly, and the distance between them is
measurable here. Every expression we emit assumes a metric type the query does not
state; the repository the dashboards come from declares that type for $89.5\%$ of them,
and our inference is wrong on $6.5\%$ of those --- $491$ occurrences, against an
inexpressible column with nothing in it. The translations are exact, and exact relative to context
the query omits; no coverage number can see that, which is why $\kappa$ is not this
paper's main result (\S\ref{sec:emission}, \S\ref{sec:caveats}).

Coming back is a different matter, and here the obstructions are proved. A Datadog tag
filter is a glob, and \S\ref{sec:regular} shows a glob's language is either a single word
or unbounded in length --- from which, together with the fact that a glob star admits
every character, it follows that no glob denotes a single arbitrary character, nor the
non-empty values, nor a wildcard confined to part of the alphabet. A fourth result
generalises the last of those: an \emph{infinite} language that never uses some character
of the alphabet is no union of finitely many globs, however many branches are spent.
Infinite is a hypothesis and not a flourish --- a finite language omits characters and is
a union of globs, one per word --- and this sentence dropped it for eleven passes. Together with a
bound on union size that we choose and state, these account for every refusal in that
section --- $8.1\%$ of the corpus's matcher occurrences whose pattern is concrete ---
and the fourth carries $10$ of them with no bound at all.

Put together with the directional barrier of \S\ref{sec:ingestion}, the picture is an
asymmetry: leaving costs engineering, returning costs expressiveness. The asymmetry binds
differently from the way lock-in is usually described, and \S\ref{sec:discussion} is
careful about it. What we measured cheap is the exit. What is expensive is the return, so
the cost falls on a team that has already left and wants back, or on one keeping the
option open --- which is a cost of \emph{irreversibility} rather than of captivity, and
it is paid by a different decision at a different time.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{What a dashboard query does not say (\S\ref{sec:caveats},
\S\ref{sec:dashboard-layer}).} The paper's main result, and the one the other seven were
built to reach without knowing it. Every translation carries the preconditions it rests
on, so the share depending on context the query omits is counted rather than conceded:
$97.0\%$ on a rollup interval, $100\%$ on a metric type, against $55.6\%$ of PromQL
range selectors taking their window from a dashboard variable --- which makes the
contrast between the languages one of degree and locates the cost in neither of them.
One of the two assumptions is checkable against the vendor's own declarations and is
wrong for $6.5\%$ of the cases where it can be checked, the first number here that
separates coverage from correctness.
\item \textbf{Grammars (\S\ref{sec:grammars}).} Deterministic one-token-lookahead
grammars for both languages, including one for Datadog's metric query language, which
the vendor does not publish and which had to be inferred from documentation and
$8{,}770$ vendor-authored queries. The Datadog grammar is conflict-free LALR(1); the
PromQL one has $2$ shift/reduce conflicts, which we show come from the optionality of
the vector-matching label list rather than from how we layered the rules --- not that no
LALR(1) grammar for PromQL exists, a claim \S\ref{sec:grammars} declines to make.
\item \textbf{$\phi$-relative soundness (\S\ref{sec:ingestion}).} Soundness stated
relative to an ingestion mapping, with the consequence that the information barrier
between these two systems is \emph{directional}. The reverse half is
Proposition~\ref{prop:reverse}: a PromQL query that distinguishes two databases Datadog
stores identically has no sound Datadog translation, quantified over \emph{every}
Datadog query rather than over the output of any one translator, so it is a result about
the languages and not about our engineering.
\item \textbf{Exact rollup translations (\S\ref{sec:ingestion}, \S\ref{sec:translation}).}
Datadog's \texttt{.as\_count()} and \texttt{.as\_rate()} translated exactly, not merely
to within $\varepsilon$, with the preconditions named; and soundness theorems whose
hypotheses are the preconditions the emitter reports, plus three it does not record
and the paper therefore states instead, covering first the kernel
($95.7\%$ of the corpus) and then its arithmetic closure --- which is every expression
this translator emits.
\item \textbf{The regular sub-problem (\S\ref{sec:regular}).} Glob-expressibility of a
regular expression is decidable, by an argument about shortest words, and the
impossibility results above. The construction that makes most of it work is one fact used
in both directions.
\item \textbf{A gap catalogue, split (\S\ref{sec:gaps}).} Every refusal carries a code,
and the catalogue separates three things most translation work runs together: what the
target system does not \emph{hold}, what PromQL cannot \emph{say}, and what we have not
\emph{built}. The first two were occupied for most of this paper's life and hold, at the
end of it, nothing this paper can show belongs there --- $14$ occurrences aside, refused
on a hypothesis it can test for the metrics the vendor declares and not for theirs.
Keeping the three apart is what made each departure a finding rather than a revision, and
what makes the near-empty column reportable at all.
\item \textbf{Coverage on real dashboards (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}).} $\kappa$ over the
whole corpus, by occurrence rather than by distinct string, with the implementation order
that produced it and the features that cost the remainder.
\item \textbf{A measured baseline (\S\ref{sec:baseline}).} Another vendor's
Datadog-to-PromQL translator, run over this same corpus --- the first such comparison we
know of, and possible only because its translation turns out to run in the browser. It
refuses nothing and emits for every occurrence, yet $5.3\%$ of what it emits is not
PromQL, and it returns an expression for all $275$ queries this paper refuses, the
percentiles among them.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{A note on method.}
One discipline runs through all of it and is worth stating in advance, because this paper
got it wrong eight times. A translator's refusal looks like a fact about the languages and
is in the first instance a fact about the translator. Separating the two takes something
independent: a proof that no target expression exists, a decision procedure that agrees,
or an implementation that removes the refusal and so demonstrates it was never a gap.
An earlier version of this work refused $348$ query occurrences on a commutation argument
that the real evaluation order never raises; a later one reported all $124$
disjunctive scopes as a language gap when $120$ of them were a single regex matcher away;
a third called \texttt{.weighted()} inexpressible for $70$ occurrences when the modifier
turns out to compute nothing at all; a fourth counted the four disjunctions no
single key covers as beyond PromQL two paragraphs from the sentence explaining how
PromQL says them; a fifth called $36$ zero-fills inexpressible on the strength of
one candidate expression, when a real server supplies the value per series for another
(Remark~\ref{rem:fill}); a sixth filed $31$ occurrences of a construct this paper's own
normaliser had manufactured; a seventh refused $34$ percentile queries under a corollary
whose hypothesis none of them meets; and an eighth refused $5$ scopes for a distinction
the target's data model defines away, which is the reason the exact translation is to
drop the term. All eight
would have produced a stronger-sounding claim, and all eight erred in the same direction.
The last four are the ones that should be least reassuring. The first four surfaced
while something else was being measured, which is luck; these four came from going
looking --- the zero-fills because a reviewer asked why two rows of one column had
proofs and two had sentences, and the three after them because that question was then
put to every remaining row, and every remaining row answered it badly.
\S\ref{sec:gaps} and \S\ref{sec:glob-corpus} report which refusals now have which
warrant.
