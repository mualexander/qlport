\section{Grammars (RQ1)}
\label{sec:grammars}

Both languages are context-free, which is the premise Rich's §48.1 supplies for treating
query languages as formal languages at all~\citep{rich2008}. The sharper question, and
the one RQ1 asks, is whether each admits a \emph{deterministic} parser with one token of
lookahead --- an LALR(1) grammar. A positive answer buys two things the rest of the paper
needs: the grammar is unambiguous, so a query has exactly one parse tree to translate;
and parsing is linear, so the corpus of \S\ref{sec:evaluation} can be processed in bulk.

The answer differs by language, and the difference is a finding rather than an artefact
of how we wrote the rules. The inferred Datadog grammar is conflict-free. PromQL is not,
and the conflicts it has are forced by the language as specified.

\subsection{Core productions}

Figure~\ref{fig:productions} gives the shape of each grammar in Rich's notation,
abridged to the productions that carry the structure; the full grammars are
\texttt{src/qlport/grammars/*.lark}.

\begin{minipage}{0.47\linewidth}
\textbf{Datadog (metrics)}
\begin{lstlisting}
query     -> spaceagg? ':'? metric scope
             grouping? method*
spaceagg  -> sum | avg | min | max
           | count | pNN
scope     -> '{' sexpr '}'
sexpr     -> sexpr 'or' sand | sand
sand      -> sand (',' | 'and') snot | snot
snot      -> ('!' | 'not') snot | satom
satom     -> tag | '(' sexpr ')' | member
member    -> TAG 'not'? 'in'
             '(' tag (',' tag)* ')'
grouping  -> 'by' '{' KEY (',' KEY)* '}'

\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.47\linewidth}
\textbf{PromQL}
\begin{lstlisting}
expr    -> or_e
or_e    -> or_e 'or' mod and_e | and_e
and_e   -> and_e SETOP mod cmp_e | cmp_e
cmp_e   -> cmp_e CMPOP mod add_e | add_e
add_e   -> add_e ADDOP mod mul_e | mul_e
mul_e   -> mul_e MULOP mod unary | unary
unary   -> ADDOP unary | pow_e
pow_e   -> postfix POW mod unary | postfix
postfix -> atom
         | postfix '[' DURATION ']'
         | postfix '[' DURATION ':'
                       DURATION? ']'
         | postfix 'offset' ADDOP? DURATION
         | postfix '@' at_arg
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\caption{Core productions. PromQL's operator precedence is encoded in the layering of
\texttt{or\_e} through \texttt{pow\_e}, lowest binding first, as in Rich's
Example~11.19; \texttt{mod} carries the optional \texttt{bool} and vector-matching
modifiers. Datadog's scope is a boolean query over tags, layered the same way so that
\texttt{and} binds tighter than \texttt{or}.}
\label{fig:productions}
\end{figure}

Encoding precedence in the rule levels rather than in a separate precedence declaration
is what keeps the grammar unambiguous by construction: a flat
$\mathit{expr} \rightarrow \mathit{expr}\ \mathrm{OP}\ \mathit{expr}$ has no
deterministic parse, and a parser generator asked to build it must either report a
conflict or silently pick one reading. Two further points of Figure~\ref{fig:productions}
are worth drawing out, because both were forced by the corpus rather than by the
documentation. Datadog's scope is not a list of tag terms but a boolean query over them,
with comma and \texttt{and} as the same operator (\S\ref{sec:datadog-inference}). And the
space aggregator is optional: a bare metric name is a legal query, which the published
examples never show.

\subsection{Is the table conflict-free?}

Table~\ref{tab:grammar-stats} reports the constructed tables. A methodological note
first, because the answer depends on it: the parser generator we use (Lark) reports
shift/reduce and reduce/reduce conflicts, and collisions between terminals, only in a
strict mode that is off by default. With it off, conflicts are resolved silently by
preferring a shift, so a grammar that builds has demonstrated nothing about ambiguity.
The last column of Table~\ref{tab:grammar-stats} is therefore produced under strict
mode; the parsers used elsewhere in this work keep the permissive mode deliberately, for
the reason given in \S\ref{sec:promql-conflict}. That the strict check is doing real work
is itself checked: perturbing either grammar into an ambiguous production, or adding a
terminal that matches the same strings as an existing one, is caught in both cases.

\subsubsection{The PromQL conflicts, and their one cause}
\label{sec:promql-conflict}

PromQL's table carries $2$ conflicts, both a shift/reduce on the left parenthesis, and
neither is an accident of our encoding. They sit in two states --- one reached after
\texttt{group\_left} and one after \texttt{group\_right} --- and have a single cause, so
an earlier version of this subsection reported one conflict rather than two. That was not
a reading of the table: Lark raises at the \emph{first} conflict in strict mode, so the
message the measurement returned said one however many there were, and the count is now
read off the resolution log instead (\texttt{parse.py}'s
\texttt{lalr\_conflict\_report}). The cause is that the vector-matching modifiers take an
\emph{optional} parenthesised label list, so after either of them an opening parenthesis
may begin that list or a parenthesised right operand:
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\texttt{a * on(x) group\_left (y) b}\qquad vs.\qquad\texttt{a * on(x) group\_left (b)}
\end{center}
One token of lookahead cannot distinguish them at that point, and the conflict comes
from the optionality rather than from our layering: making the label list mandatory
removes it entirely, which is how we identify the cause, but that also rejects
\texttt{group\_left b}, which the language allows. What this shows is that \emph{this}
grammar is not LALR(1), not that no LALR(1) grammar for PromQL exists --- the language
is not ambiguous, since the expression above has one parse, which Prometheus produces
and then rejects for want of an operand. We make no claim about the whole class of
grammars for this language; a formulation that defers the decision past the
parenthesis may well exist.

The resolution is to shift, reading the parenthesis as the label list. That rejects
\texttt{a * on(x) group\_left (b)}, even though a reader can see the intended parse, and
it is not a liberty we take: Prometheus~3.15.0 rejects the same expression by the same
route, and accepting it would make this grammar \emph{more} permissive than the language
it describes (\apref{app:proofs}).

\iffull\input{sections/moved/gr_conflict_resolution}\fi

So RQ1's answer for PromQL is ``LALR(1) modulo one documented resolution'', not
``conflict-free'' --- one resolution, applied in both states, and the one the reference
implementation already makes. We draw nothing further from it. A tie-break on an optional
parenthesised label list in the \emph{target} language costs a migration nothing, no
corpus query is affected, and an earlier version of this section offered it as evidence
for the paper's thesis, which it is not.

\subsection{Lexical decisions}

Keyword-rich query languages put most of the difficulty in the lexer rather than the
parser, and both grammars needed terminals shaped by word boundaries, lookaheads and
priorities. Table~\ref{tab:grammar-decisions} records them the same way
\S\ref{sec:datadog-inference} records the missing productions: each decision is
perturbed back to the form it would naively take, and a witness is re-parsed to show
what breaks. A row that still parsed would mean the decision was dead weight; none is.
The witnesses are minimal queries written for the table rather than corpus queries, which
an earlier version of this paragraph claimed they were; what the corpus supplies is the
count beside each one, of distinct corpus queries the real grammar parses and the naive
form does not. Every decision is load-bearing against the corpus as well as against its
witness, and the Datadog colon lookahead is the row where the two differ in kind: no
corpus query names a metric beginning with an aggregator word, and what the corpus
exhibits instead is a \emph{function} whose name does, \texttt{count\_not\_null(...)}.

\subsection{Inferring the Datadog grammar}
\label{sec:datadog-inference}

PromQL has a published grammar to compare against~\citep{promql}. Datadog's metrics
query language has none, so its grammar has to be inferred and the inference has to be
checked. We did it in two stages, and report both, because the gap between them is the
measurement.

Grammar v1 was written from the documentation and the published examples alone, before
the corpus existed. Running it over the corpus (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}) left a
substantial share of real queries unparsed, and the question is what to conclude from a
failure. Rather than classify failures by hand, each is explained by the smallest set of
\emph{repairs} that makes it parse, where every repair is one concrete production or
terminal change. Grammar v2 is v1 with all of those changes applied. Each row of
Table~\ref{tab:grammar-gaps} is therefore verified twice: the construct fails under v1
and is explained by exactly that repair, and v2 parses the same construct with no repair
at all. The residual --- queries no combination of repairs explains --- is reported
rather than absorbed.

Two results in Table~\ref{tab:parse-coverage} matter beyond the grammar. First, the
documentation was a poor guide to the language: v1 parsed $92.6\%$ of the instantiated
\emph{distinct} Datadog queries and v2 parses $99.9\%$, and the constructs in between
are not exotic ---
the single most common, boolean scopes, appears in $139$ distinct queries, and a metric
with no aggregator prefix in $93$. A grammar inferred from a vendor's own documentation
misses a measurable fraction of the vendor's own shipped dashboards.

Second, the gap between the raw and instantiated columns is large in both languages:
$11.8\%$ against $99.9\%$ for Datadog, $71.2\%$ against $99.1\%$ for PromQL. Every rate
in that table is a share of \emph{distinct} queries, and the reason is worth stating
because coverage in \S\ref{sec:evaluation} is deliberately not: a grammar either accepts
a string or does not, so counting the same string forty times would measure the
dashboards rather than the language, while a migration that must rewrite forty panels
by hand is counting panels. Both denominators appear in this paper and each claim says
which it uses. The reason
is that dashboard queries are not queries but \emph{templates} --- $88.6\%$ of Datadog and
$82.5\%$ of PromQL query \emph{occurrences} contain at least one variable such as
\texttt{\$host} or \texttt{\$\_\_rate\_interval}, so these two shares are counted the
other way from the parse rates beside them, and both are the same quantity only after a
correction (\apref{app:eval}). A translator for dashboards therefore takes a query
schema, not a query, and \S\ref{sec:evaluation} measures coverage on the instantiated
form for that reason.

What remains unparsed is not a grammar gap. The PromQL residual is almost entirely
queries in which a variable is substituted into the middle of an identifier, so that no
fixed string is a query at all; the Datadog residual is a single undocumented construct,
the conditional aggregator \texttt{count(v: v>=0):}, which appears in Datadog's own
shipped dashboards. Inventing a production for an undocumented construct would be worse
than reporting it.

\subsection{What the corpus says the languages are}

A grammar describes what is sayable; the corpus says what is said, and the two are far
apart in both languages. Constructs the grammars above must accept turn out to be all
but unused --- subqueries appear in $1.1\%$ of PromQL queries and the \texttt{@}
modifier in none of the $8{,}096$ --- while the constructs that dominate are very few:
\texttt{sum:} and \texttt{avg:} are $95\%$ of Datadog's space-aggregator occurrences,
and \texttt{rate} and \texttt{irate} are $69\%$ of PromQL's function calls.

The asymmetry cuts in opposite directions for a parser and for a translator. A grammar
must cover the tail or it is not a grammar of the language, which is what the strictness
of this section buys; a translator need not, and so the question of how much of a real
estate ports is a question about the distribution rather than about the grammar.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} measures it there, and finds coverage a staircase rather
than a curve.
