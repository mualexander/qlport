\section{Evaluation (RQ3)}
\label{sec:evaluation}

RQ3 asks how much of a real dashboard estate the translator of
Section~\ref{sec:translation} actually ports. The measure is coverage $\kappa$: the share
of Datadog query \emph{occurrences} for which the translator emits a PromQL expression
rather than $\bot$. Occurrences rather than distinct strings, because a widget repeated
across forty dashboards is forty panels a migration has to rebuild by hand if it fails.

Everything in this section is computed from one frozen corpus, over the whole of it, with
nothing sampled and nothing hand-classified. Queries the grammar of
Section~\ref{sec:grammars} cannot parse stay in the denominator, so $\kappa$ is not
reported against the part of the corpus that happened to cooperate.

\label{sec:corpus}

Three sources, each pinned to a commit or a retrieval date, chosen to put a vendor's own
queries and a community's queries on the same footing
(Table~\ref{tab:corpus-sources}).

\texttt{kube-prometheus} supplies PromQL written and maintained by the Kubernetes

language is published (Section~\ref{sec:datadog-inference}) and vendor-authored queries

Extraction yields $22{,}903$ query strings. Nine turn out to be LogQL --- Loki panels
in dashboards registered under a Prometheus datasource --- and are dropped rather than
counted as PromQL the grammar fails on, leaving $22{,}894$ in three populations:
$8{,}770$ Datadog metric queries, $8{,}102$ PromQL expressions ($7{,}867$ panels, $139$
alerting rules, $96$ recording rules), and $6{,}022$ Datadog formula expressions.
Three of those counts moved once the extractor was read rather than trusted, and
Remark~\ref{rem:extraction-fixes} reports them with what the extractor still does not
reach.

The formula population fell by nearly half for a different reason, and not one the

\paragraph{Instantiation.}
Dashboard queries are not queries. A panel ships \texttt{\$pod},
\texttt{\$\_\_rate\_interval} or \texttt{\$host} where a value belongs, and
Table~\ref{tab:parse-coverage} already showed what that costs: $11.8\%$ of distinct
Datadog and $71.2\%$ of distinct PromQL queries parse as shipped, against $99.9\%$ and
$99.1\%$ once instantiated. The third of those substitutions is not a detail on the
PromQL side: an earlier version of this paragraph described only the first two and said
everything else was left alone, and the two it described reach $95.8\%$ rather than
$99.1\%$. \texttt{corpus.normalise} performs the substitution a dashboard performs
--- a PromQL range variable becomes a duration, a Datadog scope variable becomes
\texttt{*}, a variable anywhere else outside a string literal becomes an identifier, and
a variable \emph{inside} one is left alone, because \texttt{\{pod=\~{}"\$pod"\}} already
parses --- and every measurement below is made on the
instantiated form. The consequence for RQ2 is not cosmetic: the object a dashboard
migration translates is a query \emph{schema}, and a translator that takes query strings
is solving a different problem from the one its users have.

\subsection{Coverage \texorpdfstring{$\kappa$}{kappa}}

Table~\ref{tab:translation-coverage} reports the outcome of lowering and emitting every
instantiated Datadog query in the corpus: $8{,}770$ occurrences, $7{,}690$ distinct after
instantiation. (Table~\ref{tab:parse-coverage} counts $7{,}799$ distinct, because it
counts distinct \emph{shipped} strings, before two templates collapse onto one query.)

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\footnotesize
\input{tables/translation_coverage}
\caption{Translation outcomes over the Datadog corpus. Every $\bot$ carries a gap code
and appears in Table~\ref{tab:gap-catalogue}; the unparsed row is kept in the denominator
so that $\kappa$ is a statement about the corpus rather than about the parsable part of
it.}
\label{tab:translation-coverage}
\end{table}

\noindent
$\kappa = 96.6\%$ of occurrences, and $96.5\%$ of distinct queries. The two agreeing to
within a tenth of a point is worth a sentence, because there was no reason they should:
it says the queries the translator refuses are neither more nor less repeated across
dashboards than the ones it handles. There is no cheap win hiding in the popular queries, and conversely no
consolation that the failures are all one-offs.

Two sanity conditions hold on the translated set. Every emitted expression parses under
the PromQL grammar of Section~\ref{sec:grammars} --- all $7{,}576$ of them, since $89$
of the $7{,}422$ translated \emph{distinct} queries are comma-separated lists that emit
one expression per series. Those two counts are by distinct query rather than by
occurrence, because what is being checked is a property of each emitted string. That is checked as an assertion rather than reported as a rate, since the
only acceptable number of syntactically invalid outputs is zero.

\shortonly{%
That bound has a shape, and the shape is a result in its own right. Coverage is
conjunctive over features --- a query translates only when \emph{every} feature it uses is
supported --- while popularity is marginal, so the coverage a fragment buys does not grow
with the fragment. Taking Datadog's features in greedy order, the best six reach $1.6\%$
of the corpus, of which $1.1\%$ is queries that use no feature at all; the next two,
template-variable scopes and \texttt{sum:}, take it to $49.2\%$; and reaching $90.9\%$
needs sixteen of the $28$. PromQL behaves the same way and slightly worse. For anyone
building one of these translators that is the paper's practical finding --- a partial
translator is worth almost nothing, because no prefix of the feature list buys
proportional coverage --- and it is why the bound below is read off the translator's own
feature set rather than off the curve. \apref{app:eval} gives both orders and both curves.

}
The feature bound of \S\ref{sec:staircase} applies to $\kappa$, and applying it correctly
is a correction rather than a calculation. The translator's own feature set is the one to
use: it supports $S^\star$, the $25$ features that appear in some query it translates, and
every query using a feature outside $S^\star$ is refused by construction, so the share of
occurrences whose features all lie inside it --- $97.3\%$ --- bounds $\kappa$ from above.
$\kappa$ is $96.6\%$, and the gap is $56$ occurrences that use nothing outside $S^\star$
and are refused anyway --- $87$ before the normaliser stopped truncating a template
variable at its hyphen, since $31$ of the $87$ were the velero panels that mangling
refused. Those are not unattempted easy queries. They are queries the
feature vocabulary cannot tell apart from ones that translate: \texttt{.fill(0)} and
\texttt{.fill(null)} are both \texttt{M\_FILL}, and a conjunctive and a disjunctive
boolean scope are both \texttt{SCOPE\_BOOL}, so the bound's slack measures the resolution
of the instrument rather than idleness in the translator.

An earlier version of this paragraph read the bound off the greedy curve instead, placing
$\kappa$ between the values at twenty-six and twenty-seven features and concluding that
the translator sat at its frontier. Both halves were wrong. Greedy gives an attainable set
at each size rather than an optimal one, so its value is a \emph{lower} bound on the best
a fragment of that size could reach and a real translator may sit above it --- as this one
does, covering $97.3\%$ with $25$ features where the greedy value at $25$ is $96.2\%$. The
curve of \S\ref{sec:staircase} answers a different question, which is what to implement
next, and it answers that one well.

\subsection{The gap catalogue}
\label{sec:gaps}

Table~\ref{tab:gap-catalogue} is the other half of RQ2's answer: not ``what fraction
fails'' but ``which constructs, and why''. Every row is reached by a query a real
dashboard ships, and every $\bot$ carries one code from \texttt{diagnostics.py}.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\scriptsize
\input{tables/gap_catalogue}
\caption{The gaps, by occurrence. ``Share'' is of all Datadog query occurrences in the
corpus.}
\label{tab:gap-catalogue}

The division the catalogue was built for is between what the target cannot
\emph{hold}, what it cannot \emph{say}, and what we have not \emph{built}. The division
is still the right one and the answer it now returns is not the one this paper spent
most of its life reporting: on this corpus, in this direction, the first two kinds hold
nothing shown to be beyond reach. All $287$ refused occurrences --- $3.3\%$ --- are
unwritten code, with one qualification this section states where it arises and carries
everywhere the column is mentioned: $14$ of them are refused on a hypothesis this paper
tests for the declared metrics and cannot test for theirs, so they are undecided rather
than retracted.

Six rows left those two columns, and the order matters because the last three are not
findings about Datadog at all.

\texttt{SCOPE\_DISJUNCTION} went first, when \S\ref{sec:disjunction} showed a union of
selectors says what a disjunctive scope says; what refuses it is the syntax-directed
shape of this translator, not PromQL. \texttt{.weighted()} went next, when the modifier
turned out to compute nothing (Remark~\ref{rem:weighted}). \texttt{FILL\_INTERPOLATION}
($36$) went third: the argument was that no PromQL term supplies a value where the
database holds none, which is true of \texttt{or vector(0)} --- the one candidate it
tried --- and false of the claim, because
\texttt{m or (last\_over\_time(m[W]) * 0)} keeps a stopped series alive through the
window and reports zero for it, per series and with its labels
(Remark~\ref{rem:fill}).

\texttt{LABEL\_NAME\_GLOB} ($31$) went fourth and was never a Datadog construct.
\texttt{corpus.\_TMPL} matched a template variable only as far as its first hyphen, so
the scope
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\texttt{\{\$cluster-location,\$cluster-name,\$cluster-uid,\$host\}}
\end{center}
arrived at the translator as \texttt{\{*-location,*-name,*-uid,*\}} --- a string
Datadog's own query parser rejects, which Remark~\ref{rem:keyless-glob} reads off the
account. This paper
invented a semantics for that string, proved something about it, and filed $31$
occurrences as beyond the reach of any engineering. Instantiated correctly the scope is
four match-all terms and the query translates without comment.

The fix is deliberately asymmetric: in PromQL a hyphen is subtraction, so widening the
pattern there would read \texttt{\$a-b} as one variable where it means a difference. Six
PromQL occurrences carry the same shape and are left as written, and nothing rests on
it --- the value sits inside a string literal, which the normaliser never enters, so
\S\ref{sec:regular} counts all six as template-valued and never asks what they denote.

\texttt{PHI\_QUANTILE} ($34$) went fifth, and it is the one that was proved.
Corollary~\ref{cor:quantile} assumes a Datadog \emph{distribution} metric, which answers
$\mathtt{p}NN$ from a sketch no bucketing determines. The corollary is sound, and the
refusal fires on seeing \texttt{p}$NN$\texttt{:} without testing what the corollary
requires --- but how often the hypothesis actually fails is settled for some of the
occurrences and not all. \texttt{integrations-core} declares $23{,}597$ \texttt{gauge},
$13{,}140$ \texttt{count} and $330$ \texttt{rate} metric names, three that two
integrations declare incompatibly, and not one \texttt{distribution} among all
$37{,}070$. $20$ of the $34$ name a metric in that set, so for those the hypothesis
provably fails.

The other $14$ name metrics the repository never declares, and an earlier version of
this paragraph wrote ``none of the $34$ is one'', which reads the declared set as a
census of the language. It is not one. Those $14$ are five names, all from the APM and
LLM-observability families rather than from an integration:
\texttt{ml\_obs.span.duration} and \texttt{data\_streams.payload\_size} at four
occurrences each, \texttt{airbyte.metrics\_reporter.overall\_job\_runtime\ldots} at
four, and \texttt{trace.langchain.request} and \texttt{trace.openai.request} at one
each. A latency percentile over spans is exactly the quantity a sketch is for, so these
are the occurrences where the corollary's hypothesis is most likely to hold, and nothing
in the corpus or in this paper's experiments settles whether it does. They are reported
as undecided for the same reason \S\ref{sec:conclusion} reports the $736$ occurrences
whose type the repository does not declare: the instrument reaches the declared set and
the population is larger than the declared set.

So the surviving column is $20$ occurrences refused on a hypothesis shown to fail and
$14$ on a hypothesis not tested either way. That is weaker than this paper claimed and
it is the honest shape of it, and the measurement that would close it is one request per
name to the vendor's metric-metadata endpoint, which needs an account carrying APM data
rather than the integration dashboards this corpus came from.
\texttt{SCOPE\_EMPTY\_VALUE} ($5$) went with it: Prometheus drops an empty-valued label
at ingest, so no database it holds --- and so no image of $\phi_\rho$ --- distinguishes
an absent tag from a present-empty one, and the exact translation of \texttt{!k:} is to
drop the term. The same remark had already excused $120$ occurrences of \texttt{k:*}
under precisely that condition and called refusing them ``the overcautious reading''.

So the catalogue's own structure survives and its contents do not. What that leaves is a
stronger claim than the one this paper set out to defend, and a weaker instrument for
making it: \S\ref{sec:discussion} takes up both.

\paragraph{What the one surviving column contains.}
\texttt{UNSUPPORTED\_FUNCTION} is almost all of
it, led by \texttt{top(...)} at $139$ occurrences, which is expressible after all
(\apref{app:eval}): PromQL denotes Datadog's ranking with an \texttt{@}-pinned
\texttt{topk}, read off a real server, and the vendor's own \texttt{top()} was then
measured to behave as its documentation says. Of the rest, the $36$ constant fills and
the four surviving disjunctions are sayable by constructions this paper has written out,
and the $9$ occurrences of \texttt{\{*,!*\}} turn out not to need one:
Remark~\ref{rem:negated-all} reports the account selecting the series for both
\texttt{\{*,!*\}} and \texttt{\{!*\}}, so the term is not the empty selection the
catalogue assumed and the translation is to drop it. Every refusal this emitter raises
is now code nobody has written, and most of it is code whose shape is known.

\subsection{Exact relative to what}
\label{sec:caveats}

Every translation this paper calls exact is exact relative to stated preconditions, and
Table~\ref{tab:translation-caveats} counts how often each is in play. These are not
hedges added after the fact: each is a diagnostic the emitter attaches to the expression
it returns, and the shares are over the translated set.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\footnotesize

\texttt{ROLLUP\_ALIGNMENT} is universal because Datadog's buckets are epoch-aligned
while a PromQL range window follows the evaluation step, so the two systems agree bucket
for bucket on the aligned grid and not off it (Section~\ref{sec:ingestion}).
\texttt{ROLLUP\_DEFAULT} covers the $97.0\%$ of translations whose source query never
stated a rollup interval, because Datadog derives it from the dashboard's time range ---
information the query text does not contain. This is the single largest qualification on
$\kappa$ and it is a property of the source language, not of the translator: a Datadog
query is not self-contained, so a translated query cannot be either.

The rest of the table is four codes and one conclusion.
\texttt{COUNTER\_RESET} and \texttt{PHI\_SERIES\_START} each mark the same $23.7\%$ ---
the translations using the cumulative-difference form of
Proposition~\ref{prop:offset} --- for two conditions that coincide in this corpus
without being the same condition, and that the anchored form of
Proposition~\ref{prop:anchored} separates. \texttt{TAG\_PRESENCE\_EMPTY} marks the
$1.4\%$ whose selector asks a tag merely to be present, where the three tag states
Datadog distinguishes collapse to two in Prometheus's data model --- the same barrier
that refuses \texttt{SCOPE\_EMPTY\_VALUE} outright, appearing here as a condition on the
target database rather than as a refusal. \texttt{METRIC\_TYPE\_UNKNOWN} marks
$100\%$ of them, for the reason \S\ref{sec:emission} gives, and it is the one
precondition here whose violation rate can be measured: on the $89.5\%$ of translated
occurrences whose type the vendor's own repository declares, the front end's inference
is wrong for $6.5\%$. So $\kappa$ counts $491$ translations that are, on that
declaration, wrong. What the repository declares is what an integration \emph{submits}, so
a disagreement says the inference is wrong and not that it must be --- a dashboard can
query a metric the integration does not ship, and an operator can submit the same name as
something else. The declaration is a sharper instrument than the query text it replaces,
not a proof (Remark~\ref{rem:type-measured}). $\kappa$ is a coverage measure and never was
a correctness measure, and this is the first number in the paper that says how far apart
the two are (Remark~\ref{rem:type-caveat}).

The conclusion is the one an earlier draft of this section had backwards. That draft said
$116$ occurrences carried \texttt{ROLLUP\_ALIGNMENT} alone and so rested on nothing but
the evaluation grid; Remark~\ref{rem:counter-caveats} separates the two conditions that
coincide here without being one. With \texttt{METRIC\_TYPE\_UNKNOWN} recorded the true figure is
\emph{none} --- every one of those $116$ is an \texttt{avg} rollup --- and once the
modifiers' evidential value is withdrawn as well, no translation in this corpus is exact
on the strength of anything the query itself contains. That is a stronger claim than the
one it replaces, and it was invisible only because a precondition was.

\subsection{Every shape, against both systems}
\label{sec:differential}

The construction is the one Section~\ref{sec:replay} applies to a single trace, applied
instead to every distinct query shape among the corpus occurrences this translator
emits a single expression for. For each shape the harness
reads off the lowered IR which series the query's own filters select, synthesises them,
writes the cumulative counter to a local Prometheus and its image under $\phi$ to a real
Datadog account, then evaluates the Datadog query against Datadog and its translation
against Prometheus --- on the bucket grid that query's own rollup interval defines --- and
compares the two readings bucket by bucket
(\texttt{scripts\slash shape\_differential.py}; cached and regression-tested).


It is a smaller experiment than it looks, and the obvious estimate of its cost is badly
wrong. What a differential test exercises is the \emph{operator structure} --- which
rollup, which aggregation, which matcher shapes --- so two queries differing only in the
metric they name are one experiment rather than two. Abstracting away the identifiers and
nothing else, keeping everything a representative would emit differently, collapses the
$8{,}370$ translated occurrences that are a single query to \textbf{$333$ distinct
shapes} (Remark~\ref{rem:shape-reduction} on what the reduction is and is not),
instantiated as $888$ synthetic series in all --- a scale one trial account
sustains, which is why the experiment turned out not to need a reconstruction of
somebody's production estate. The reduction is sound only if every query in a class
translates the same way, and that is checked rather than argued: emitting all of them and
masking the identifiers leaves each class with exactly one form, so a representative
stands for its class (\apref{app:eval}).

\iffull\input{sections/moved/eval_shape_reduction}\fi

Table~\ref{tab:differential} reports the outcome. Of the $333$ shapes, $330$ could be
compared, and all $330$ agreed with Datadog on every one of $22{,}692$ bucket
comparisons --- to float64 precision rather than bit-identity, since two engines summing
the same values in a different order differ in the last bit, and the bound is the one the
model-versus-server check of Section~\ref{sec:ingestion} already uses; the largest
deviation over all $330$ is $3.5\times10^{-14}$ and $137$ of them are bit-identical.
Weighted by the corpus queries those shapes stand for, that is $8{,}365$ of the
$8{,}370$ translated single-query occurrences. So all but five of $\kappa$'s single-query
occurrences are now translations whose arithmetic has been run against the vendor rather
than only emitted, which is a claim this paper could not previously make at all. It is
worth saying precisely, since the looser version of it --- ``checked against the
vendor'' --- claims the validation the paragraph above denies: what was run is the
arithmetic, on idealised series, under every condition the translation could have been
wrong about.

This is the third run of the experiment, and that matters more than the agreement does.
The first was made on the corpus before \texttt{corpus.\_TMPL} was fixed, and
\S\ref{sec:threats} concedes that a rig which has been wrong three times in the
agreement-flattering direction reports $329$ of $329$ in the version that passes. The
second was on a corrected corpus with a fresh set of synthetic series, and returned the
same $329$ of $329$ over the same $22{,}608$ buckets with a bound an order of magnitude
tighter. This one draws a third independent set of series and carries the grouping-key
fix described below, and returns $330$ of $330$ over $22{,}692$ buckets. Independent
replication was the thing \S\ref{sec:threats} asked for and did not have; there are now
two, and neither changed a verdict. The second of them moved one shape out of the
uncompared column, which is the only status any shape has changed across three runs.

Three shapes of the $333$ are not compared: two carry a rollup interval the run's
half-hour of synthetic points cannot hold and one returned no data on either side, and all three were in the first run
too. A fourth was uncompared in the second run, and resolving it is why there is a third.
It came back \emph{unmatched} --- both systems returned data and no bucket
could be paired, because Datadog had grouped by \texttt{host} alone where the emitted
PromQL grouped by all four keys the query names. That was not a translation defect: it
was \texttt{shape\_differential} submitting the synthetic tags under their normalised
spelling while asking Datadog to group by the query's own, which the harness maps for
dotted keys and did not for hyphenated ones. The mapping now comes off the parse tree
rather than a regex, and on this run the shape compares: $84$ buckets, every one of them
bit-identical. It is no longer the one shape in the corpus whose translation has never
been checked against either system, and the $8{,}365$ above includes it. The prediction
that it would compare was made before the run and is the only thing here that could have
come out otherwise.

What the run is worth is what it found, and reporting only the agreement rate would hide
it. Four translation defects survived the unit tests and the single-trace replay and were
caught here. A rollup other than $\mathsf{sum}$ over a metric \emph{known} to be a counter
emitted an \texttt{*\_over\_time} of the cumulative series, which is not a function of the
deltas Datadog reduces --- wrong output rather than output with an unmet precondition,
since the type was not in doubt. Underneath it, \texttt{.as\_count().rollup(avg, 60)} was
losing the type evidence altogether, because the kind was read off the \emph{final} time
aggregator and the later \texttt{.rollup} overwrote it. Third, \texttt{.as\_rate()} was
emitted inside the space aggregation; that is invisible for $\mathsf{sum}$,
$\mathsf{avg}$, $\mathsf{min}$ and $\mathsf{max}$, which commute with scaling, and the
one $\mathsf{count}$ shape in the corpus disagreed by exactly a factor of $\Delta$
(Remark~\ref{rem:as-rate-placement}). Fourth, a binary operation between a grouped and an ungrouped
vector was emitted with PromQL's default matching, which pairs nothing and returns the
empty vector where Datadog broadcasts --- silently, since an empty result is not an
error; the two corpus occurrences now carry an explicit matching clause. None of the four
is a gap in either language. All four are translator defects of the kind that a coverage
number cannot see, because a wrong translation and a right one are both translations.
What the run does not cover is Remark~\ref{rem:differential-limits}.

Six systematic failures came out of this rig, three before any of those four defects was
visible at all and three more as the run was tightened, and every one of the six was the
harness rather than the systems (Remark~\ref{rem:harness-failures} lists them): in each
case a parameter was read from somewhere other
than the place that stated it, which is \S\ref{sec:caveats}'s sentence about a rig
instead of a query (\apref{app:eval}). Six instrument faults to four real defects is the
honest summary of the exercise, and a more useful lesson about differential rigs than the
agreement rate is.

The run leaves three of the $333$ shapes uncompared and its series are synthetic, both
of which \apref{app:eval} sets out. The sharper limit is which hypotheses the run can
reach, and the honest answer is none of them: all six of
Theorem~\ref{thm:kernel}'s hypotheses hold by construction, and so do both of its
side conditions. An earlier version of this paragraph named two and called them ``the
two that matter most'', which was true of those two and silent about the other four.
The evaluation instant is floored to a multiple of $300$\,s and read at
$b+\Delta-\varepsilon$, so (1) holds; the query range is fixed at four hours precisely
because E4 measured four hours as the $60$\,s the emitter assumes, so (2) holds; each
metric is submitted under the kind the \emph{translator inferred} from the query text,
so (3) holds; the counter values are a cumulative sum of non-negative steps and never
reset, so (4) holds; the first bucket of the window is skipped, so every compared bucket
has a sample before it and (5) holds; and no series carries an empty tag value, because
the rig writes none, so (6) holds. The grid is a regular $15$\,s one with no gaps, so the lookback condition holds,
and the identifiers are synthetic while the shape abstraction masks metric names and tag
keys, so the three real mangling collisions of Remark~\ref{rem:mangling} cannot arise
either.

Stated in full, that is still the right design, and for the reason the shorter version
gave: a run in which the hypotheses were already false would measure the hypotheses
rather than the arithmetic. What it is not is a check on $\kappa$. Given
Theorem~\ref{thm:kernel}, $330$ of $330$ is what the theorem \emph{predicts}, so the
run's value is not the agreement but the four translator defects it found on the way to
it --- \texttt{.as\_count().rollup(avg,60)} losing its type evidence, \texttt{.as\_rate()}
inside the space aggregation, the \texttt{*\_over\_time} of a cumulative series, and the
unmatched binop --- none of which the theorem predicted and each of which was a real
error in the emitter. A differential run against a live vendor is how those were found;
the agreement afterwards is a consistency check on the arithmetic. The conditions
\S\ref{sec:discussion} argues are the real switching cost are exactly the ones the rig
holds true, so the run is silent on what happens when they fail ---
Remark~\ref{rem:type-cost} measures that separately, and measures it as unbounded in the
counter's age.

\subsection{A baseline: what another translator does}
\label{sec:baseline}

$\kappa$ has nothing to be compared against in the literature (\S\ref{sec:background}),
but it does have something to be compared against in practice. OpenObserve ships a
dashboard migrator whose Datadog-to-PromQL translation runs in the browser, so it can be
driven over this corpus directly; the bundle is cached with its hash and retrieval date
because the service is hosted and unversioned, and
\texttt{scripts/openobserve\_baseline.py} reproduces everything below. Queries whose
source is several comma-separated metrics are held aside --- $117$ occurrences, where our
emitter returns a list and theirs one string --- leaving $8{,}653$.

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} is the comparison, and it does not run in the direction a
coverage number would suggest.
Their translator refuses nothing: it emits an expression for every one of the $8{,}653$
occurrences, against our $96.7\%$. But $457$ of those --- $5.3\%$ --- are not PromQL.
$235$ carry Datadog's match-all scope \texttt{\{*\}} into a language where \texttt{*} is
multiplication, $111$ are the Datadog text passed through unchanged, and $111$ fail for
other reasons. Every expression we emit parses, under this paper's grammar and under
Prometheus's: the two instruments refuse the same $386$ expressions and accept the same
rest, so the column is not an artefact of measuring PromQL with our own ruler. So on the
one measure both tools can be
held to without first agreeing on semantics --- coverage by \emph{valid} output --- the
figures are $94.7\%$ against $96.7\%$, and the translator that refuses more emits more.

What happens to the queries we refuse is the sharper result. All $275$ of them receive
an expression, across every one of the five gap codes. The $34$ percentile aggregators
become \texttt{quantile(0.95, \ldots)} across series, which is a quantile of the
\emph{series} rather than of the distribution; the $36$ \texttt{.fill} interpolations
and the $5$ empty-valued scopes become expressions with the construct removed. None of this is concealed
--- the output carries a note --- but a note is not a code. $68.6\%$ of occurrences carry
only the cosmetic observation that dots became underscores, so the $31.3\%$ that say
something substantive arrive in the same channel as the noise and cannot be counted
without being read.

Two of the figures are this paper's own thesis arriving from outside it. $525$
occurrences have a Datadog modifier silently dropped ($570$ drops in all: $316$
\texttt{.rollup}, $184$ \texttt{.fill}, $70$ \texttt{.weighted}), and dropping a
\texttt{.rollup} does not approximate the query, it answers a different one. And
$1{,}844$ occurrences --- $21.3\%$ --- are given a \texttt{[5m]} window no query states,
under the note ``adjust interval as needed''. That is
\S\ref{sec:dashboard-layer}'s finding reached independently by somebody who was not
looking for it: the interval is not in the query, so a translator must be told it, record
that it guessed, or guess quietly. Theirs guesses and mentions it in prose; ours records
\texttt{ROLLUP\_DEFAULT} and counts it. How the comparison was run, and what it is not
evidence of, is Remark~\ref{rem:baseline}.

One of the three dropped modifiers is dropped correctly. \texttt{.weighted()} computes
nothing (Remark~\ref{rem:weighted}) --- which this paper established only after calling
it inexpressible for $70$ occurrences --- so there their silent drop is right and our
earlier refusal was wrong.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{The corpus is published dashboards.}
Vendor and community dashboards may be tidier and more heavily templated than
in-house ones. The template-variable share ($88.6\%$ of Datadog, $82.5\%$ of PromQL occurrences)
is the figure most likely to be inflated this way; $\kappa$ is less exposed, since the
gaps it reports are structural constructs rather than stylistic ones. A corpus of
private dashboards would be the better test and was not obtainable for this work; that
is a statement about our access, not about whether such a corpus could exist.

\paragraph{The Datadog half of the corpus has one source.}
All $8{,}770$ Datadog occurrences come from \texttt{integrations-core}: one organisation's
queries in one house style, with nothing to pair against the PromQL side's two independent
sources, because no comparable public body of customer-written Datadog dashboards exists.
\S\ref{sec:heldout} does find a second public body that is not customer-written ---
\texttt{integrations-extras}, the community- and partner-maintained sibling --- and holds
it out. That bears on the fit most directly, but it bears on this paragraph too, because
the two repositories' house styles are measurably different: $88.6\%$ of corpus
occurrences carry a template variable against $77.0\%$ of the sibling
repository's occurrences.
Table~\ref{tab:heldout}'s raw column is the same fact counted the other way round and
over distinct strings rather than occurrences --- a Datadog string parses as shipped
exactly when it carries no variable, with two exceptions in the whole held-out set ---
so $11.8\%$ of corpus strings parsing as shipped against $22.8\%$ there is a restatement
of the template share and not a second channel of evidence for it. One measurement,
then, and it confirms the template share as source-specific, in the direction the
paragraph above fears. What neither repository is,
is evidence about what customers write.
The choice was deliberate --- no reference grammar is published and vendor-authored queries
are the closest thing to a specification by example (\S\ref{sec:corpus}) --- but a
specification by example is not a sample of what customers write, so \S\ref{sec:gaps}
reports which constructs appear in Datadog's own dashboards rather than which appear in
anyone's. It bears on \S\ref{sec:caveats}'s $491$ most sharply, because the declarations
the inference is checked against come from the same repository as the queries: the
disagreement is measured between two artefacts by the same authors. That is a strong
signal about the inference, which ought to agree with them and does not, and no evidence
about how often it fails on the metrics this repository never mentions --- $736$
occurrences, which Remark~\ref{rem:type-measured} counts but cannot check.

\paragraph{The grammar and the implementation order were fitted to this corpus.}
Three places it matters should be named together, and then separated, because they are
not all fitted in the same way and all three turn out to be testable --- two by refitting
them on part of the corpus, the third by freezing it and reading it somewhere else.
Grammar v2 is v1 plus every production the corpus showed was missing
(\S\ref{sec:datadog-inference}), and Table~\ref{tab:parse-coverage} measures the size of
that fit rather than hiding it: $92.9\%$ of distinct instantiated Datadog queries parsed
before the corpus was consulted and $99.9\%$ after. The translator's feature set is the
$25$ features that appear in some query it translates. And \S\ref{sec:staircase}'s
implementation order is a greedy maximum-coverage order computed \emph{over this
corpus}. So $\kappa=96.6\%$ is a resubstitution estimate: it is coverage after
development was driven by these dashboards, and the honest reading of it is ``what this
corpus asks for, once someone has read it'' rather than ``what a dashboard estate should
expect''. The one-decimal place is a property of the arithmetic, not a claim to that
precision out of sample.

It bears on \S\ref{sec:baseline} hardest, and in the direction that favours this paper:
OpenObserve's translator never saw this corpus, while this paper's grammar was extended
until it parsed it, so the $96.7\%$ against $94.7\%$ of that comparison is an in-sample
number beside an out-of-sample one.

\S\ref{sec:heldout} takes the two remedies this paragraph used to defer --- a disjoint
set of dashboards, and a later commit of the same repository, with the grammar and the
translator frozen --- and reports what they show. The short form: the feature vocabulary
and the implementation order are functions of the corpus, so they can be refitted on part
of it and scored on the rest, and both broadly generalise: the vocabulary saturates
after a tenth of the dashboards, missing one aggregator on the disjoint repository, and
the build order loses one point out of sample. The grammar
is not refittable by any script, so it was frozen and read on dashboards nobody here had
seen. On $497$ community dashboards the corpus never listed --- the $12{,}522$ distinct
strings there whose instantiated form the corpus does not hold --- v2 parses $99.6\%$
against $99.0\%$ in sample, and its margin over v1 is $0.8$ points against $1.2$ here. On
$2{,}572$ distinct queries from \texttt{integrations-extras}, a repository in the same
organisation by other authors that the corpus does not touch, v2 parses $99.9\%$ --- its
in-sample rate --- and its margin over v1 is $5.6$ points against the seven of the climb
from $92.9\%$ to $99.9\%$ above. Neither of those falls is what it looks like. Both are
differences between populations rather than measurements of overfitting: on the Datadog
side v2's two rates agree to four significant figures, so essentially the whole of the
fall is the unfitted v1 doing better out of sample, and on the PromQL side both grammars
do better out of sample, v2 included. And neither fall is separable from no fall at all
--- a dashboard-level jackknife puts $0.4$ points of spread on the first and $1.9$ on
the second, each at least as large as the fall beside it. What survives on both sides is the
held-out margin itself. And
$\kappa$ itself, which the paragraph above this one said had no holdout, is
$96.7\%$ of those $3{,}065$ occurrences against $96.6\%$ here --- the same figure as
\S\ref{sec:baseline}'s coverage by valid output above, and an unrelated quantity, and a
tenth of a point where dropping dashboards one at a time moves the held-out figure by
$1.1$ --- refusing under
four of the five gap families \S\ref{sec:gaps} catalogues. So the estimate is still a
resubstitution estimate, and the worry it licenses is bounded instead of open. On the three
channels \S\ref{sec:heldout} can read twice --- the PromQL grammar, the Datadog grammar
and $\kappa$ --- the margin the corpus bought is still there out of sample, how much of
it the corpus kept for itself is smaller than the movement either figure carries across
dashboards, and on $\kappa$ the two readings cannot be told apart. Nothing is read twice
on the other channels: the differential, the replay and the trial probes all run against
one account's data and have no holdout at all. Two caveats are left and neither is leakage --- those
dashboards are built with the vendor's own scaffolding against the vendor's own
documentation, and their strings are novel while $90.0\%$ of their occurrences reuse a
feature set the corpus already holds --- so the corpus of private, customer-written
dashboards this subsection asks for is still the test that was not run.

\paragraph{Everything here is about the query layer.}
What we measure is the cost of rewriting saved queries, and \S\ref{sec:discussion}'s
conclusions about where the switching cost sits are conclusions about that layer. A real
migration also re-instruments services, re-models metric cardinality and cost,
re-establishes retention, and rebuilds alerting and on-call integration, and none of
those is measured here or follows from anything that is. The claim that the dashboard
layer is where the cost concentrates is a claim \emph{within} the query problem, not a
ranking of it against the rest of a migration.

\paragraph{$\kappa$ is measured on one direction and one language pair.}
Datadog metrics $\to$ PromQL. Remark~\ref{rem:directional} argues the reverse direction
is harder for reasons that do not depend on the corpus, but it is not measured, and
nothing here extends to logs or traces.

\paragraph{Occurrence weighting flatters nothing here, but it could.}
Weighting by occurrence is the right measure for a migration and the wrong one for a
language comparison. Both are reported in Table~\ref{tab:translation-coverage} and they
agree to within a tenth of a point, so the choice happens not to matter; in a corpus
where a single refused widget were duplicated across hundreds of dashboards it would
matter a great deal.

\paragraph{One ingestion path.}
Section~\ref{sec:ingestion}'s $\phi$ models the agent's cumulative-to-delta conversion.
OTLP ingestion applies the same cumulative-to-delta rule but maps metric temporality,
type and datapoint attributes by its own table~\citep{ddotlp}, so which series exist
and what kind each one is are properties of a configured pipeline rather than of
``Datadog''. A migration on a different path inherits the framework but not the constants.

\paragraph{The automatic rollup interval is assumed, not derived.}
$\kappa$ is computed with a $60$\,s default where a query states no interval, and the
$97.0\%$ figure in Table~\ref{tab:translation-caveats} is the size of that exposure. An
earlier version of this paragraph called the assumption ``systematic rather than
arbitrary'' because Datadog publishes the interval as a time-range lookup rather than a
formula, which is true and is not a defence of the constant.
Remark~\ref{rem:rollup-spans} measures the lookup at all ten spans the corpus's own
widgets declare and finds $60$\,s at exactly one of them: four hours, which $28.3\%$ of
those widgets ask for. The modal span is one hour at $38.3\%$, where the interval is
$20$\,s, so the default is not merely unverified --- it is wrong for most of the corpus's
widgets that say, and wrong by a factor of three for the commonest of them. A translator
for a specific dashboard must read the interval off that dashboard's time range, and the
same remark shows it cannot recover it from the response instead.

RQ4 asks how often a translator supplying the obvious default is wrong, and the two
halves of that answer were on disk for some time before being joined. For the metric
type it is $6.5\%$ and $491$ occurrences, quoted throughout this paper. For the
interval, joining each translated occurrence to its widget's declared span and that span
to E18's lookup gives this. Of the $8{,}217$ occurrences carrying
\texttt{ROLLUP\_DEFAULT}, $496$ --- $6.0\%$ --- sit in a widget that pins its own span,
and for those the emitter's $60$\,s is the interval Datadog would have used for $164$ of
them and the wrong one for $332$, or $66.9\%$. The modal declared span is one hour,
$205$ occurrences, where Datadog rolls up at $20$\,s and the emitted \texttt{[60s]}
answers a question three times as wide; the declared spans run from one minute, where
$60$\,s is $60$ times too wide, to one week, where it is a fifty-ninth of the interval
Datadog uses. Every span the corpus declares is one E18 measured, so none of this rests
on interpolation.

The other $7{,}721$ cannot be decided, and the distinction matters. A widget that pins
nothing takes its span from the dashboard's time selector, which is a viewer's choice at
view time and appears nowhere in the JSON --- so for those the default is
\emph{unverified}, which is what this paragraph claimed, rather than wrong. Carrying the
measured share across all $8{,}217$ would give about $5{,}500$, and that number is an
extrapolation and should not be read as a second measurement: an author who pins a span
is doing something deliberate, most plausibly because the dashboard's default was wrong
for that panel, so the widgets that declare are if anything enriched for disagreement
and $5{,}500$ is an upper estimate.

What this does not do is move $\kappa$. The $60$\,s is a placeholder for computing
coverage, every occurrence that carries it is counted as translated either way, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:kernel}'s hypothesis~(2) is stated in terms of ``the width of the
bucket Datadog reports'' rather than in terms of $60$\,s --- so a translator that reads
the span off the dashboard discharges the hypothesis and emits a different constant,
without changing which queries translate. The figure is the size of the correction a
real per-dashboard translator makes.

Set beside the type default, the comparison is not the one the raw counts suggest. The
type default is wrong for $491$ of the occurrences it can settle and the interval default
for $332$, which look alike --- but the denominators differ by an order of magnitude. The
type can be settled for $89.5\%$ of translated occurrences and is wrong for $6.5\%$ of
them; the interval can be settled for $6.0\%$ and is wrong for $66.9\%$ of those. So the
default this paper has been quoting is the one that is almost always right on a
population that is almost always decidable, and the one it had not computed is wrong two
times in three on a population that is almost never decidable. That is the sharper
statement of \S\ref{sec:discussion}'s claim that the language pillar is the heavier of
the two: not that it produces more errors, but that it is the one a translator cannot
check.
