\subsection{Disjunctive scopes}
\label{sec:disjunction}

A Datadog scope is a boolean query over tags; a PromQL selector is a \emph{conjunction}
of matchers. Conjunction, negation and glob matching therefore cross over term by term,
and disjunction has no selector-level form --- in general. It has one in the case that
matters:

\begin{proposition}[Single-key disjunction]\label{prop:disjunction}
Let $k$ be a tag key, let $g_1,\dots,g_n$ be Datadog globs or literals, and let $r_i$ be
the regular expression $\mathrm{glob}(g_i)$ of \S\ref{sec:regular}. Then the scope
$\{k{:}g_1 \vee \cdots \vee k{:}g_n\}$ --- equivalently the membership form
$\{k \in (g_1,\dots,g_n)\}$ --- denotes the same series set as a single PromQL matcher on
$k$ whose regular expression is the alternation $r_1|\cdots|r_n$; the negated scope is
the same alternation under \texttt{!\textasciitilde{}} instead of
\texttt{=\textasciitilde{}}.
\end{proposition}

Given the semantics of alternation the only thing to check is where the anchors go, and
that is a question about Prometheus rather than about this translator:
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\texttt{\textasciicircum(?:aws|gcp)\$} \quad denotes $\textstyle\bigcup_i L(r_i)$
\qquad versus \qquad
\texttt{\textasciicircum aws|gcp\$} \quad which also admits \texttt{awsx}
\end{center}
Prometheus compiles the first. Since asserting that would be asserting what we want, it
is read off a real server: the matcher \texttt{k=\textasciitilde{}"aws|gcp"}, over series
carrying \texttt{aws}, \texttt{azure}, \texttt{gcp}, \texttt{awsx} and \texttt{xgcp},
selects exactly the first and third --- and its negation selects the other three
\emph{and} a series carrying no \texttt{k} at all, which is what the negated Datadog
scope means (\texttt{scripts/verify\_matchers.py}, Prometheus~3.15.0, with the responses
recorded and regression-tested).

Two details decide how much of the corpus this reaches. Datadog's \texttt{or} binds
more loosely than its comma, so \texttt{\{a:x or a:y,*,*\}} parses as
$a{:}x \vee (a{:}y \wedge * \wedge *)$ and the right branch is a \emph{conjunction} ---
one whose match-all terms contribute nothing, leaving a single constraint. Lowering each
branch before asking whether it is one constraint, rather than requiring a bare term,
is what admits those. And a branch that merely asks for the tag to be present subsumes
every value, so the union collapses to
\texttt{k=\textasciitilde{}".+"} rather than growing an alternative.

What remains refused is disjunction across \emph{different} keys, which
\S\ref{sec:gaps} measures at four query occurrences in the corpus. Those stay sayable in
PromQL --- as a set union of selectors with the rollup distributed over the branches,
$2^k$ selectors for $k$ disjunctions --- but not as the syntax-directed translation of a
scope, which is what this translator is.

\subsection{Where the rollup sits}
One structural fact about Datadog decides the shape of every translated query, and it is
easy to get backwards. A Datadog query is evaluated ``first by time, then by
space''~\citep{datadog}: the rollup reduces each series on its own, and the space
aggregator (\texttt{sum:}, \texttt{avg:}, \texttt{max:}, \texttt{min:},
\texttt{count:}, with its optional \texttt{by \{...\}}) then combines those reduced
values. PromQL's range functions have the same shape, so the translation is direct:
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\texttt{max:x\{*\}.rollup(avg, 60)}\quad$\longmapsto$\quad\texttt{max(avg\_over\_time(x[60s]))}
\end{center}

Nesting the two layers the other way round --- space aggregation first, rollup outside
--- describes a different query, and raises a question the real semantics never asks:
whether the two aggregators commute. They do for $(\mathsf{sum},\mathsf{sum})$,
$(\mathsf{max},\mathsf{max})$ and $(\mathsf{min},\mathsf{min})$, and not for
$\mathsf{max}$ or $\mathsf{count}$ over an averaging rollup, where PromQL would need a
subquery to put the rollup outside. An earlier version of this translator was built that
way and refused $348$ query occurrences --- four points of $\kappa$ --- on the strength
of that commutation argument. Every translation it \emph{did} emit was still correct,
because where the aggregators commute the two orders coincide, which is exactly what
makes the error hard to notice: the failure shows up only in the refusals.
