\section{Translation (RQ2)}
\label{sec:translation}
RQ2 asks for the largest fragment that translates soundly, which presupposes something
to be sound against. This section supplies it: an intermediate representation with a
denotation, a lowering from the source language into it, an emission from it into the
target, and a statement of what the composite guarantees. The gap catalogue is in
\S\ref{sec:gaps} and the coverage in \S\ref{sec:evaluation}; what belongs here is the
machinery and the one theorem it is for.

The translator is the classical three-stage shape --- parse, lower, emit --- for the
classical reason: it is the only arrangement in which $n$ front ends and $m$ back ends
cost $n+m$ pieces of work instead of $nm$~\citep{aho2006}. That matters here beyond
economy. A direct Datadog-to-PromQL rewriter would have no object to be sound
\emph{about}, so every claim would have to compare two vendor implementations; with an
IR in between, soundness factors into two claims against one definition.

\subsection{A typed series algebra}
\label{sec:ir}

\begin{definition}[IR]\label{def:ir}
The IR is two-sorted. Sort $\mathsf{S}$ is a \emph{set of series}, sort $\mathsf{M}$ a
\emph{set of windowed sample sequences}, and sort $\mathsf{V}$ a \emph{vector}: a finite
set of label-set/value pairs with distinct label sets. The terms are
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{0.40\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Sort & Term & \\
\midrule
$\mathsf{S}$ & $\mathrm{Select}(m,\overline{\mu},\kappa)$
  & metric name, label matchers, kind $\kappa$ \\
$\mathsf{M}$ & $\mathrm{Window}(e^{\mathsf{S}},w)$ & the samples in $(t-w,t]$ \\
$\mathsf{V}$ & $\mathrm{RangeFn}(f,e^{\mathsf{M}})$ & a function of a window \\
$\mathsf{V}$ & $\mathrm{Rollup}(\mathit{op},\Delta,e^{\mathsf{S}},b)$
  & Datadog's time aggregation; $b$ divides by $\Delta$ \\
$\mathsf{V}$ & $\mathrm{Agg}(\mathit{op},\overline{k},\mathit{mode},e^{\mathsf{V}})$
  & space aggregation, keeping or dropping $\overline{k}$ \\
$\mathsf{V}$ & $\mathrm{BinOp}(\mathit{op},e^{\mathsf{V}},e^{\mathsf{V}},\overline{k})$
  & arithmetic, matched on $\overline{k}$ \\
$\mathsf{V}$ & $\mathrm{Scale}(c,e^{\mathsf{V}})$, $\;\mathrm{Number}(c)$ & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Here $\kappa\in\{\text{counter},\text{gauge},\text{unknown}\}$,
$\mathit{op}\in\{\mathsf{sum},\mathsf{avg},\mathsf{min},\mathsf{max},\mathsf{count}\}$,
and a matcher $\mu$ is one of $k=v$, $k\neq v$, $k=\!\sim\! r$, $k\neq\!\sim\! r$.
A term is \emph{well sorted} if each argument has the sort its position requires; only
$\mathsf{V}$ terms are complete queries.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Denotation]\label{def:ir-sem}
Fix a database $D$ and a time $t$. Writing $\lambda(s)$ for a series' label set and
$s|_{(a,b]}$ for its samples in that interval:
\begin{align*}
\sem{\mathrm{Select}(m,\overline{\mu},\kappa)}(D,t)
  &= \{\, s\in D \;:\; \mathrm{name}(s)=m,\ \lambda(s)\models\overline{\mu} \,\}\\
\sem{\mathrm{Window}(e,w)}(D,t) &= \{\, (\lambda(s),\ s|_{(t-w,\,t]}) \;:\; s\in\sem{e}(D,t) \,\}\\
\sem{\mathrm{Rollup}(\mathit{op},\Delta,e,b)}(D,t)
  &= \{\, (\lambda(s),\ \mathit{op}(s|_{[t,\,t+\Delta)})\,/\,\Delta^{[b]}) \;:\; s\in\sem{e}(D,t) \,\}
\end{align*}
where $\Delta^{[b]}$ divides only when $b$ (Datadog's \texttt{.as\_rate()}) holds. For
space aggregation let $\pi_{\overline{k}}$ keep the labels in $\overline{k}$ under
$\mathit{mode}=\mathsf{by}$ and drop them under $\mathsf{without}$, and let $G_\lambda$
collect the values of $\sem{e}(D,t)$ whose label set agrees with $\lambda$ on
$\overline{k}$; then
\[
\sem{\mathrm{Agg}(\mathit{op},\overline{k},\mathit{mode},e)}(D,t)
  = \{\,(\pi_{\overline{k}}(\lambda),\ \mathit{op}(G_\lambda))\,\}.
\]
$\mathrm{Number}$, $\mathrm{Scale}$ and
$\mathrm{BinOp}$ are pointwise on matching label sets. A matcher is satisfied as
PromQL satisfies it, with $=\!\sim$ anchored over the whole value
(\S\ref{sec:disjunction}).
\end{definition}

\noindent
Two features of Definition~\ref{def:ir} are load-bearing rather than tidy. First,
$\mathrm{Rollup}$ is the composite of $\mathrm{Window}$ and a reduction, which is why it
goes from $\mathsf{S}$ to $\mathsf{V}$ in one step: Datadog has no term for a window on
its own, so an IR that separated them would offer the front end a choice the source
language does not have. $\mathrm{Window}$ and $\mathrm{RangeFn}$ remain because PromQL
does separate them, and the Datadog-to-PromQL emitter accordingly refuses both --- they
are reserved for the reverse direction in the same way as the unreachable codes of
\S\ref{sec:gaps}.

Second, the sorts make \S\ref{sec:ingestion}'s Corollary~\ref{cor:raw} structural.
A complete query has sort $\mathsf{V}$, and the only terms of sort $\mathsf{S}$ are
$\mathrm{Select}$s, which can appear solely as the argument of a $\mathrm{Window}$ or a
$\mathrm{Rollup}$. So a well-sorted IR term \emph{cannot} present a bare selector to the
emitter: the corollary said no sound translation reads absolute counter values, and the
type discipline is what enforces it, with no check to forget to run.

\subsection{Where the rollup sits}
\label{sec:rollup-order}
One structural fact about Datadog decides the shape of every translated query, and it is
easy to get backwards. A Datadog query is evaluated ``first by time, then by
space''~\citep{datadog}: the rollup reduces each series on its own, and the space
aggregator (\texttt{sum:}, \texttt{avg:}, \texttt{max:}, \texttt{min:},
\texttt{count:}, with its optional \texttt{by \{...\}}) then combines those reduced
values. PromQL's range functions have the same shape, so the translation is direct:
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\texttt{max:x\{*\}.rollup(avg, 60)}\quad$\longmapsto$\quad\texttt{max(avg\_over\_time(x[60s]))}
\end{center}

Nesting the two layers the other way round --- space aggregation first, rollup outside
--- describes a different query, and raises a question the real semantics never asks:
whether the two aggregators commute. They do for $(\mathsf{sum},\mathsf{sum})$,
$(\mathsf{max},\mathsf{max})$ and $(\mathsf{min},\mathsf{min})$, and not for
$\mathsf{max}$ or $\mathsf{count}$ over an averaging rollup, where PromQL would need a
subquery to put the rollup outside. An earlier version of this translator was built that
way and refused $348$ query occurrences --- four points of $\kappa$ --- on the strength
of that commutation argument. Every translation it \emph{did} emit was still correct,
because where the aggregators commute the two orders coincide, which is exactly what
makes the error hard to notice: the failure shows up only in the refusals.

\begin{remark}[Where \texttt{.as\_rate()}'s divisor sits]\label{rem:as-rate-placement}
The rollup is inside the space aggregation and the modifier's division is not, which is
a second ordering question and was answered the same way round as the first: wrongly, at
first, and then by measurement.

\texttt{.as\_rate()} divides by the rollup interval. Dividing by a positive constant
commutes with $\mathsf{sum}$, $\mathsf{avg}$, $\mathsf{min}$ and $\mathsf{max}$, so for
those the divisor can sit on either side of the space aggregation and nothing
distinguishes the readings. It does not commute with $\mathsf{count}$, which counts
series and is blind to their values: $\mathrm{count}(x/\Delta) = \mathrm{count}(x)$
while $\mathrm{count}(x)/\Delta$ is not. So one operator decides the question for all
of them, and asking it of a real account answers it ---
\texttt{count:m\{*\} by \{g\}.rollup(avg, 30)} returns $1$ per group and the same
query with \texttt{.as\_rate()} returns $1/30$
(\texttt{scripts\slash as\_rate\_order.py}). Datadog divides the aggregated result, so
the emitter places the divisor outside the aggregation for $\mathsf{count}$ and, for
readability, leaves it inside where it commutes.

The same run settles the modifier's other ordering, against an explicit
\texttt{.rollup}. Submitting every $10$\,s and rolling up over $30$\,s separates
dividing each delta by its own submission interval from rolling up and dividing once by
the bucket: the first reads $1$, the second $10/30$. Datadog reads $10/30$. Both
readings coincide whenever the submission grid equals the rollup width, which is every
harness in this project and the reason the question went unasked for so long.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Lowering}
\label{sec:lowering}

Lowering is a syntax-directed translation in the sense of \citet{lewis1968}: one bottom-up
pass over the Datadog parse tree, each node's IR computed from its children's. Two
attributes break that pattern, and both break it because the information is not in the
query.

The first is the metric's \textbf{kind}. Whether a Datadog metric was submitted as COUNT
or GAUGE decides which rule its rollup may use, and a dashboard's JSON never records it.
What the query carries is evidence rather than a statement: \texttt{.as\_count()},
\texttt{.as\_rate()} and a \texttt{.rollup(sum, $\Delta$)} are all documented for COUNT
metrics, so the front end reads any of them as the type the query does not give. The
evidence is weaker than it looks and Remark~\ref{rem:gauge} measures how much weaker;
what matters here is the shape of the inference, not its strength. The
method chain is a \emph{sibling} of the scope, and the kind belongs to the
$\mathrm{Select}$ beneath it, so the attribute is synthesised at the query node and
inherited downward --- an attribute grammar in Knuth's sense~\citep{knuth1968} rather
than a bottom-up transduction. Absent any such evidence the kind stays $\text{unknown}$
and the back end is left to say what it rests on.

The second is the rollup \textbf{interval}. Datadog derives it from the dashboard's time
range when \texttt{.rollup} does not give one, and the query text does not contain the
time range. The IR therefore stores $\Delta=\bot$ and the back end resolves it, so
$\mathrm{Rollup}$ is the one node whose denotation is undefined until a parameter outside
the IR is supplied. This is not elegant and the alternative is worse: inventing a default
during lowering would bury an assumption in the IR, where nothing downstream could see
that it had been made.

\begin{remark}[One gap per query]\label{rem:one-gap}
Lowering abandons a query at its first untranslatable construct, and the
$\bot$ it returns carries that one \texttt{Gap}. So the catalogue of
\S\ref{sec:gaps} counts \emph{first} refusals: a query with two problems appears under
whichever is reached first, and the per-code counts are lower bounds on how many queries
each construct affects. What they are not lower bounds on is the total --- every refused
query is counted exactly once --- so $\kappa$ is unaffected, and the ordering of
\S\ref{sec:staircase} is computed from features rather than from gap codes for this
reason.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Counter rollups}
The rules for \texttt{.as\_count()}, \texttt{.as\_rate()} and \texttt{.rollup(sum,
$\Delta$)} are established in Section~\ref{sec:ingestion}, where they belong: which
PromQL expressions may translate a Datadog rollup at all is a question about the
ingestion mapping, not about the emitter. Propositions~\ref{prop:offset}
and~\ref{prop:anchored} give the two exact forms and Table~\ref{tab:rollup-live} the
measurements. What the emitter adds is the choice between them and the preconditions it
reports: \texttt{COUNTER\_RESET} on the cumulative difference,
\texttt{ANCHORED\_VERSION} on the anchored form, and \texttt{ROLLUP\_ALIGNMENT} on
both.

\subsection{Disjunctive scopes}
\label{sec:disjunction}

A Datadog scope is a boolean query over tags; a PromQL selector is a \emph{conjunction}
of matchers. Conjunction, negation and glob matching therefore cross over term by term,
and disjunction has no selector-level form --- in general. It has one in the case that
matters:

\begin{proposition}[Single-key disjunction]\label{prop:disjunction}
Let $k$ be a tag key, let $g_1,\dots,g_n$ be Datadog globs or literals, and let $r_i$ be
the regular expression $\mathrm{glob}(g_i)$ of \S\ref{sec:regular}. Then the scope
$\{k{:}g_1 \vee \cdots \vee k{:}g_n\}$ --- equivalently the membership form
$\{k \in (g_1,\dots,g_n)\}$ --- denotes the same series set as a single PromQL matcher on
$k$ whose regular expression is the alternation $r_1|\cdots|r_n$; the negated scope is
the same alternation under \texttt{!\textasciitilde{}} instead of
\texttt{=\textasciitilde{}}.
\end{proposition}

Given the semantics of alternation the only thing to check is where the anchors go, and
that is a question about Prometheus rather than about this translator:
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\texttt{\textasciicircum(?:aws|gcp)\$} \quad denotes $\textstyle\bigcup_i L(r_i)$
\qquad versus \qquad
\texttt{\textasciicircum aws|gcp\$} \quad which also admits \texttt{awsx}
\end{center}
Prometheus compiles the first. Since asserting that would be asserting what we want, it
is read off a real server: the matcher \texttt{k=\textasciitilde{}"aws|gcp"}, over series
carrying \texttt{aws}, \texttt{azure}, \texttt{gcp}, \texttt{awsx} and \texttt{xgcp},
selects exactly the first and third --- and its negation selects the other three
\emph{and} a series carrying no \texttt{k} at all, which is what the negated Datadog
scope means (\texttt{scripts/verify\_matchers.py}, Prometheus~3.15.0, with the responses
recorded and regression-tested).

Two details decide how much of the corpus this reaches. Datadog's \texttt{or} binds
more loosely than its comma, so \texttt{\{a:x or a:y,*,*\}} parses as
$a{:}x \vee (a{:}y \wedge * \wedge *)$ and the right branch is a \emph{conjunction} ---
one whose match-all terms contribute nothing, leaving a single constraint. Lowering each
branch before asking whether it is one constraint, rather than requiring a bare term,
is what admits those. And a branch that merely asks for the tag to be present subsumes
every value, so the union collapses to
\texttt{k=\textasciitilde{}".+"} rather than growing an alternative.

What remains refused is every disjunction whose branches do not all constrain
\emph{one} key --- across different keys, or within a branch that constrains two at
once --- which \S\ref{sec:gaps} measures at four query occurrences in the corpus and
sorts into its three shapes there. Those stay sayable in
PromQL --- as a set union of selectors with the rollup distributed over the branches,
$2^k$ selectors for $k$ disjunctions --- but not as the syntax-directed translation of a
scope, which is what this translator is.

\subsection{Emission, and conditional exactness}
\label{sec:emission}

Emission is structural recursion on the IR with no choices left to make, save one: the
reset-aware \texttt{anchored} form of \S\ref{sec:ingestion} or the plain cumulative
difference. The output is not a string but a pair.

\begin{definition}[Emission]\label{def:emission}
$E(e)$ is either $\bot$ with one gap, or a pair $(p,\,C)$ of a PromQL expression and a
set $C$ of \emph{caveats}: conditions under which $p$ is exact, which the emitter
observed itself needing and which the IR does not record as satisfied.
\end{definition}

\noindent
The caveats are not a disclaimer. They are the hypotheses of
Theorem~\ref{thm:kernel} below, collected by the only component in a position to know
which ones a given query incurred, and \S\ref{sec:caveats} reports how often each is
incurred. Five arise:
\texttt{ROLLUP\_ALIGNMENT} on every rollup, since Datadog's buckets are epoch-aligned,
left-closed and named by their start (Remark~\ref{rem:closure}) while a PromQL window is
right-closed and follows the evaluation step;
\texttt{ROLLUP\_DEFAULT} when the interval was assumed;
\texttt{COUNTER\_RESET} on the cumulative difference, or \texttt{ANCHORED\_VERSION} on the
form that avoids it; \texttt{PHI\_SERIES\_START} on the forms that read a counter, which
need a sample older than the bucket and which the anchored form again avoids
(Remark~\ref{rem:series-start}); and \texttt{METRIC\_TYPE\_UNKNOWN}, which deserves its
own paragraph.

\begin{remark}[The assumption that underwrites all of the output]%
\label{rem:gauge}
Writing Theorem~\ref{thm:kernel} down is what exposed this. An
$\mathit{op}\in\{\mathsf{avg},\mathsf{min},\mathsf{max},\mathsf{count}\}$ rollup reduces
the points Datadog \emph{stores}. For a GAUGE those are the samples Prometheus also
holds, and \texttt{avg\_over\_time(x[$\Delta$s])} is exact. For a COUNT they are
per-interval deltas, and averaging deltas is not averaging running totals --- no
\texttt{*\_over\_time} of the cumulative series computes it, and the emitted expression is
not approximately right but unrelated. The dashboard never says which, so the emission is
correct for a gauge and the emitter records \texttt{METRIC\_TYPE\_UNKNOWN} to say so. It
reports rather than refuses
because a rule \emph{can} be chosen here; for a $\mathsf{sum}$ rollup none can, and the
same code is a refusal there instead.

The condition is not a precaution. Asked of a real account, a bare query on a
\texttt{COUNT} metric returns the \emph{mean} of four stored deltas and not their sum,
so Datadog applies its enforced \texttt{avg} default rather than the \texttt{.as\_count()}
its user interface appends --- averaging deltas where \texttt{avg\_over\_time} averages
running totals (\texttt{scripts\slash trial\_checks.py e3}).

The condition was also silent until now, and it is not rare. At an earlier stage of this
work it appeared to hold of $6{,}381$ translated occurrences --- $76.2\%$ of the
translated set as it then stood, which was smaller than today's --- because Datadog's
default time aggregator is $\mathsf{avg}$ and most queries state no method at all. The remaining quarter looked exempt: a $\mathsf{sum}$ rollup needs a
counter, the ways to ask for one are \texttt{.as\_count()}, \texttt{.as\_rate()} and
\texttt{.rollup(sum, $\Delta$)}, and all three are documented for \texttt{COUNT}
metrics --- so a query using one, we reasoned, establishes the type it does not state.

It does not, and the error was of a kind this paper has made before: a rule read in a
manual and never asked of the system. Submitting a \texttt{GAUGE} and querying it with
each modifier returns the plain rollup, unchanged and undivided --- Datadog accepts both
on a gauge and silently ignores them rather than rejecting the query
(\texttt{scripts\slash as\_rate\_order.py}). The modifier therefore records what the
dashboard's author took the metric to be. It is evidence about a person, not about a
type, and no reading of the query distinguishes the two.

The third way is weaker still, and for a different reason. \texttt{.rollup(sum, $\Delta$)}
over a \texttt{GAUGE} is not ignored: the same experiment reads it as the sum of the
stored samples, which is a perfectly meaningful query that a dashboard author might well
intend. So where \texttt{.as\_count()} on a gauge is a mistake nobody benefits from,
summing a gauge over a window is an ordinary thing to want --- and the translation this
front end chooses for it, the cumulative difference, computes something else entirely.
Of the three pieces of evidence the query can carry, the one that is hardest to dismiss
as author error is the one whose misreading is least benign. With that premise withdrawn the
caveat holds of all $8{,}445$ translated occurrences, $100\%$ of them
(Table~\ref{tab:translation-caveats}).

Recording it does not move $\kappa$ --- the translations are unchanged --- which is the
point: it changes what they are claimed to be. Its refusing counterpart still never fires,
but for a weaker reason than the one we gave: not because the method establishes the type,
but because it is the only syntax that asks for the rule, so the emitter always has a rule
to choose and never has to stop. So the same unstated fact is a diagnostic that cannot
occur and a precondition that always does.
\end{remark}

\subsection{What the composite guarantees}
\label{sec:kernel-soundness}

The fragment that carries the corpus is the one \S\ref{sec:staircase} orders first:
a space aggregation of a rollup of a selector. Call it the \emph{kernel},
\[
\mathcal{K}\;::=\;\mathrm{Agg}(\mathit{op}_s,\overline{k},\mathsf{by},\
\mathrm{Rollup}(\mathit{op}_t,\Delta,\mathrm{Select}(m,\overline{\mu},\kappa))).
\]

\begin{lemma}[Scopes and selectors agree]\label{lem:scope}
For every Datadog scope $\sigma$ that lowering does not refuse, the matchers
$\overline{\mu}$ it produces select the same series as $\sigma$, provided the
name-mangling of \S\ref{sec:lowering} is injective on $D$'s label sets.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Term by term. A literal tag is an equality matcher; a negated tag its negation; a glob is
Proposition~\ref{prop:glob-regex}; a bare key or \texttt{key:*} is presence, which is
\texttt{=\textasciitilde{}".+"} and not \texttt{".*"}, the latter also matching series
carrying no such label; a single-key disjunction is
Proposition~\ref{prop:disjunction}. Conjunction is the comma in both languages.
Injectivity is needed because mangling sends \texttt{a.b} and \texttt{a\_b} to one name.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The injectivity hypothesis is not vacuous]\label{rem:mangling}
An earlier version of this proof discharged that hypothesis by asserting that no corpus
source contains such a pair. It does --- three times:
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\texttt{openstack.nova.hypervisor.load\_1} & \texttt{openstack.nova.hypervisor\_load.1}\\
\texttt{rabbitmq.queue.messages.ready} & \texttt{rabbitmq.queue.messages\_ready}\\
\texttt{cluster-name} & \texttt{cluster\_name}\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
All four queries using the two metric pairs are translated, so two distinct Datadog
metrics are emitted as one PromQL selector. The third pair is grouping keys, and it is
forced rather than incidental: a PromQL label name cannot contain a hyphen, so no
mangling avoids it.

The hypothesis is therefore a real condition on the target database and not a formality.
A translation of either \texttt{rabbitmq} query is exact in a database carrying one of
the pair and wrong in a database carrying both, where the selector would sum over series
the source query never saw. The corpus cannot say which, because a corpus of queries is
not a database. What it can say is that the collision rate is low and non-zero: three
pairs among $5{,}389$ metric names and $723$ grouping keys, pinned by test so the claim
cannot drift.

Reporting it is the same discipline as Remark~\ref{rem:refusal} applied to a hypothesis
rather than to a refusal. The assertion it replaces was checked by reading rather than by
computing, which is how a false empirical claim survived in the proof of the lemma the
soundness theorem rests on.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Kernel soundness]\label{thm:kernel}
Let $q\in\mathcal{K}$ with $E(T(q))=(p,C)$, and let $t$ be an evaluation time. If
\begin{enumerate}
\item $t$ is a multiple of $\Delta$, and the emitted expression is evaluated at
      $t+\Delta-\varepsilon$ rather than at $t$ or $t+\Delta$
      \hfill (\texttt{ROLLUP\_ALIGNMENT})
\item $\Delta$ is the interval the query states \hfill (\texttt{ROLLUP\_DEFAULT})
\item the metric's type is the one the query's methods imply: a \texttt{COUNT} where the
      query carries \texttt{.as\_count()}, \texttt{.as\_rate()} or
      \texttt{.rollup(sum,$\Delta$)}, a \texttt{GAUGE} otherwise
      \hfill (\texttt{METRIC\_TYPE\_UNKNOWN})
\item where the emitted form is the cumulative difference: no series resets in
      $[t,t+\Delta)$, or the anchored form was emitted
      \hfill (\texttt{COUNTER\_RESET}, \texttt{ANCHORED\_VERSION})
\item where the emitted form reads a counter: every series has a sample before $t$
      \hfill (\texttt{PHI\_SERIES\_START})
\end{enumerate}
and Lemma~\ref{lem:scope}'s injectivity holds, then
$\sem{q}_{\mathrm{DD}}(\phi_{\mathsf{zero}}(D),t)=\sem{p}_{\mathrm{PQ}}(D,t)$ ---
exactly, not within $\varepsilon$. Each hypothesis is an element of $C$, and $C$ contains
no others.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:scope} both systems reduce over the same set of series, so it suffices
to match the two reductions on one series $s$ and then the space aggregation.

For the time reduction, (1) makes $[t,t+\Delta)$ and the PromQL window at
$t+\Delta-\varepsilon$ cover the same samples, by the index argument of
Proposition~\ref{prop:offset}; (2) makes it the same width. Hypothesis (3) fixes
$\kappa$, and the emitter has a rule for each case it admits.

If $\kappa$ is a gauge, $\phi_{\mathsf{zero}}(s)=s$, so Datadog's stored points in the
bucket are exactly the samples $s|_{[t,t+\Delta)}$ that
\texttt{$\mathit{op}_t$\_over\_time} reduces at that instant, and the two reductions are
the same commutative operation on the same multiset.

If $\kappa$ is a counter and $\mathit{op}_t=\mathsf{sum}$, Datadog reports
$\Sigma_\Delta(t)$ and the emitted form equals it by Proposition~\ref{prop:offset}
under (4)'s first branch and (5), or by Proposition~\ref{prop:anchored} under (4)'s
second, which needs neither.

If $\kappa$ is a counter and $\mathit{op}_t=\mathsf{avg}$, Datadog reports the mean of
the deltas it stores in the bucket. The emitted form is
$\Sigma_\Delta(t)/\texttt{count\_over\_time}$, whose numerator is that sum by the
previous case and whose denominator counts the samples in the window. By (5) the series
began before $t$, so every sample in $[t,t+\Delta)$ contributes exactly one delta and
the two counts agree; the quotient is the mean.

If $\kappa$ is a counter and $\mathit{op}_t=\mathsf{count}$, Datadog reports how many
deltas it stores in the bucket and the emitted \texttt{count\_over\_time} reports how
many samples the window holds; (5) makes those the same number, by the same
correspondence. For $\mathit{op}_t\in\{\mathsf{min},\mathsf{max}\}$ over a counter no
rule exists --- the individual deltas are reachable only through a subquery over a
sample grid the query does not state --- so the emitter refuses,
$q\notin\mathrm{dom}(T)$, and there is nothing to prove.
The $\mathit{per\_second}$ divisor is $\Delta$ on both sides.

For the space reduction, Definition~\ref{def:ir-sem}'s $\mathrm{Agg}$ is what PromQL's
$\mathit{op}_s$ \texttt{by} $(\overline{k})$ computes and what Datadog's
$\mathit{op}_s{:}$ with \texttt{by \{$\overline{k}$\}} computes, on inputs just shown
equal. That $C$ contains nothing further is by inspection of the emitter: the five codes
above are the only ones it records.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Hypothesis (3) is the one that changed shape while this paper was being written, and
twice. It began as ``the metric is a gauge'', which was the only case the emitter then
handled; a counter under an averaging rollup was being emitted as
\texttt{avg\_over\_time} of the running totals, which is not a function of the deltas
Datadog reduces at all, and the corpus-scale differential test of \S\ref{sec:differential}
is what caught it. It then stopped being dischargeable from the query text, when the
modifiers it rests on turned out not to establish the type (Remark~\ref{rem:gauge}).
What it costs when it fails is measured rather than asserted: writing one stream of
counter observations to both systems and asking the commonest shape in the corpus ---
a query with no method at all --- Datadog reads the mean of the deltas, $10$ throughout,
while \texttt{avg\_over\_time} reads the mean of the running totals, climbing from $20$
to $1{,}760$ over half an hour. The relative difference runs from $0.50$ to $0.994$ and
is unbounded in the counter's age, because one quantity grows with it and the other does
not. The same run with the metric submitted as a \texttt{GAUGE}, where the hypothesis
holds, agrees exactly on every bucket (\texttt{scripts\slash type\_assumption.py}).

\noindent
The theorem is a statement about the \emph{kernel}, which is $95.4\%$ of the corpus and
$99.0\%$ of what the translator emits, but not all of it. What the remainder does is
arithmetic between kernel queries, and \S\ref{sec:binop-soundness} extends the theorem
over exactly that. The honest
summary is that the fragment the corpus overwhelmingly uses is exact under five stated
conditions, three of which are properties of the evaluation grid and the data rather
than of either language, while two --- the rollup interval and the metric's type --- are
facts a dashboard does not record. That pair is what \S\ref{sec:discussion} builds its
argument on, and the extension below adds no sixth.

That is also the answer to the question RQ2 was posed to avoid: the preconditions do not
make the translation approximate. Conditions (3), (4) and (5) are the ones that can fail
silently on real data --- the first because nothing in the query settles the type, the
other two because they are statements about data the translator never sees --- and
\S\ref{sec:caveats} measures how often each is in play.

A note on the two figures, because an earlier draft quoted a third. The $87\%$ it gave
is the cumulative column of Table~\ref{tab:implementation-order} --- the share of queries
a translator \emph{limited to the kernel's features} would complete, which is a
statement about a feature set. $\mathcal{K}$ is a statement about IR shape, and the two
differ: a query using a glob or a boolean scope is outside the first and inside the
second, since Lemma~\ref{lem:scope} covers every scope lowering admits. Computed from
the lowered IR rather than from the feature list, the kernel carries $8{,}364$
occurrences.


\subsection{Arithmetic between queries}
\label{sec:binop-soundness}

Outside $\mathcal{K}$ the corpus does one thing: arithmetic. A widget divides one
aggregated query by another, scales one by a constant, or subtracts two. Extending
Theorem~\ref{thm:kernel} over that is not a matter of applying it pointwise, because the
two languages decide \emph{which} pairs of series an operator applies to by different
rules, and neither rule is documented. Both are therefore measured.

\begin{definition}[Arithmetic closure]\label{def:arith}
$\mathcal{A}$ is the smallest set of IR terms containing $\mathcal{K}$ and every
$\mathrm{Number}(c)$, closed under $\mathrm{Scale}(c,\cdot)$ and
$\mathrm{BinOp}(\oplus,\cdot,\cdot)$ for $\oplus\in\{+,-,\times,\div\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Key set]\label{def:keyset}
$\chi(e)$ is $\star$ for $\mathrm{Number}$; $\overline{k}$ for
$\mathrm{Agg}(\mathit{op},\overline{k},\mathsf{by},\cdot)$; $\chi(e')$ for
$\mathrm{Scale}(c,e')$; and for $\mathrm{BinOp}(\oplus,l,r)$ it is $\chi(r)$ when
$\chi(l)=\star$, $\chi(l)$ when $\chi(r)=\star$, and otherwise the larger of $\chi(l)$
and $\chi(r)$ when one contains the other, and undefined when neither does.
\end{definition}

\noindent
$\chi$ is what the emitter computes, so the case analysis below is the one the code
performs rather than a description of it.

\begin{lemma}[How Datadog pairs series]\label{lem:dd-matching}
Let $q_1,q_2\in\mathcal{A}$ have results $r_1,r_2$ at $t$ with
$\chi(q_i)=\overline{k_i}$, and suppose $\overline{k_2}\subseteq\overline{k_1}$. Then
$q_1\oplus q_2$ has domain
$\{\lambda\in\mathrm{dom}(r_1) : \pi_{\overline{k_2}}(\lambda)\in\mathrm{dom}(r_2)\}$
and value $r_1(\lambda)\oplus r_2(\pi_{\overline{k_2}}(\lambda))$. If neither key set
contains the other, Datadog rejects the query.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By measurement (\texttt{scripts\slash binop\_matching.py}). Two metrics are given
groups that overlap in one value and differ elsewhere --- $a$ carries $g{=}x$ and
$g{=}y$, $b$ carries $g{=}x$ and $g{=}z$ --- so that an inner and an outer rule give
different answers. With equal key sets, $a/b$ returns $g{=}x$ alone: the groups present
on one side only are dropped, which is the first clause. With $\chi(q_2)=\emptyset$,
$a\ \mathsf{by}\ \{g\}\ /\ b$ returns both of $a$'s groups, each divided by $b$'s single
value, which is the clause at $\overline{k_2}=\emptyset$. With
$\chi(q_2)\subsetneq\chi(q_1)$ --- grouping one side by $\{g\}$ and the other by
$\{g,h\}$ --- the result is keyed by $\{g,h\}$ and matched on $g$, in both directions.
The last clause is not an inference: Datadog answers the disjoint case with
\emph{``Series to be aligned must have group keys that match, or where one set of group
keys is a subset of the other''}, which is the rule stated by the system itself.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[How PromQL pairs series]\label{lem:pq-matching}
For instant vectors $u,v$: $u\oplus v$ under default matching pairs elements with
\emph{identical} label sets and drops the rest. $u \oplus \texttt{on}(\overline{k})\ 
\texttt{group\_left()}\ v$ pairs each element of $u$ with the element of $v$ agreeing
with it on $\overline{k}$, keeps $u$'s labels, and requires at most one such $v$;
\texttt{group\_right()} is the mirror image. An aggregation drops every label outside its
\texttt{by} list, \emph{including} \texttt{\_\_name\_\_}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first three clauses are PromQL's specification~\citep{promql}, and all four were
checked against a real server on the same four series as Lemma~\ref{lem:dd-matching}. The last
is the one worth checking rather than assuming, because the whole construction depends on
it: the operands of a translated division come from \emph{different metrics}, so if
aggregation kept the metric name their label sets could never be identical and default
matching would pair nothing at all. It does not keep it, and
$\texttt{sum by (g)}(a)\,/\,\texttt{sum by (g)}(b)$ duly returns $g{=}x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Arithmetic soundness]\label{thm:arith}
Let $q\in\mathcal{A}$ with $E(T(q))=(p,C)\neq\bot$, and suppose every kernel subterm of
$q$ satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:kernel} at $t$. Then
$\sem{q}_{\mathrm{DD}}(\phi_{\mathsf{zero}}(D),t)=\sem{p}_{\mathrm{PQ}}(D,t)$, and $C$ is
the union of those subterms' caveats --- the extension introduces no condition of its
own.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Induction on the structure of $q$. For $q\in\mathcal{K}$ this is
Theorem~\ref{thm:kernel}, and for $q=\mathrm{Number}(c)$ both sides denote $c$.

$\mathrm{Scale}(c,e)$ emits $p_e * c$. Scalar--vector arithmetic is pointwise in both
languages and preserves the domain, so the claim follows from the hypothesis on $e$; the
same argument covers a $\mathrm{BinOp}$ one of whose operands is a $\mathrm{Number}$,
where $\chi$ takes the other operand's value.

For $\mathrm{BinOp}(\oplus,l,r)$ with both operands vectors, write
$\overline{k_l}=\chi(l)$ and $\overline{k_r}=\chi(r)$. By induction the two operand
results agree between the systems, so it is enough that the two languages pair them the
same way and the emitter asks for the pairing that does.

If $\overline{k_l}=\overline{k_r}=\overline{k}$, the emitter emits $p_l \oplus p_r$ with
no modifier. Each operand's PromQL result carries exactly the labels $\overline{k}$, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:pq-matching}'s last clause, so matching on identical label sets
\emph{is} matching on $\overline{k}$; the domain is the intersection and the values are
pointwise. Lemma~\ref{lem:dd-matching} gives Datadog the same intersection and the same
values.

If $\overline{k_r}\subsetneq\overline{k_l}$, the emitter emits
$p_l \oplus \texttt{on}(\overline{k_r})\ \texttt{group\_left()}\ p_r$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:pq-matching} the result is keyed by $\overline{k_l}$, each element paired
with the unique element of $p_r$ agreeing on $\overline{k_r}$ --- unique because $p_r$ is
an aggregation by $\overline{k_r}$, so its elements have distinct $\overline{k_r}$
values. That is term for term the first clause of Lemma~\ref{lem:dd-matching}. The
symmetric case emits \texttt{group\_right()}. Taking $\overline{k_r}=\emptyset$ gives the
ungrouped operand, where \texttt{on()} matches everything and the broadcast is Datadog's.

If neither key set contains the other, $\chi(q)$ is undefined, the emitter refuses and
$q\notin\mathrm{dom}(T)$. Finally $C$ gains nothing, because the emitter attaches no
caveat at a $\mathrm{BinOp}$ or a $\mathrm{Scale}$: the modifiers it chooses are decided
by $\chi$, which is a function of the query text alone.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Theorem~\ref{thm:arith} reaches further than it may look. Every term the \emph{front
end} can build lies in $\mathcal{A}$, and that is where the universal comes from ---
not from the emitter, which would happily aggregate anything handed to it, and not from
this corpus. Lowering constructs an $\mathrm{Agg}$ at exactly one place, always over a
$\mathrm{Rollup}$ of a $\mathrm{Select}$, which is $\mathcal{K}$; its only other
constructors are $\mathrm{Number}$, $\mathrm{Scale}$ and $\mathrm{BinOp}$ over terms it
has already lowered, which is the closure. $\mathrm{Window}$ and $\mathrm{RangeFn}$ are
reachable from neither, and the emitter refuses them in any case. The corpus then checks
what the construction establishes: $\mathcal{A}$ covers $8{,}445$ occurrences, $96.3\%$
of the corpus and $100\%$ of what was emitted. So the gap between what is claimed and
what is shipped is empty rather than small. That is the statement \S\ref{sec:framework}'s
Remark~\ref{rem:two-kappas} wanted and could not previously make: $\kappa$ counts
translations the emitter is willing to produce, and each of them is now covered by a
proof as well as by the corpus-scale test of \S\ref{sec:differential}.

\begin{remark}[A third kind of refusal]\label{rem:third-refusal}
The last case of Theorem~\ref{thm:arith} is a refusal of a kind \S\ref{sec:gaps} does not
have a column for. It is not a gap in PromQL, which expresses the pairing perfectly well
once asked, and it is not unfinished work here. It is a query with no source denotation:
Datadog declines to answer it. A translator cannot preserve a meaning the source system
does not assign, so the right behaviour is to refuse, and the right accounting is to
exclude it from both sides of the ledger rather than to count it against either language.
No corpus query reaches it, so nothing in \S\ref{sec:gaps} moves; the case is recorded
because the distinction is the one this paper is about and it would otherwise be filed
under ``unimplemented'' by default.

The subset case is the opposite kind of correction. An earlier version of this emitter
admitted only $\overline{k_r}=\emptyset$ and refused every other containment, which would
have been counted as unfinished work --- correctly, since the measurement shows PromQL
says it with \texttt{on($\overline{k_r}$)} and Datadog accepts it. Measuring the boundary
moved one case out of the catalogue and sharpened the other.
\end{remark}
